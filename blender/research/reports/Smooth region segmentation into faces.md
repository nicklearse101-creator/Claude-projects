# Slice the C-pillar to find its faces

Professional reverse-engineering (RE) tools do not split a smooth region with dihedral thresholds. They compute one curvature-type indicator across the feature. Its maximum marks a "smooth edge" (the blend centre), the two neighbouring minima mark the blend boundaries, and one sensitivity knob sets the scale. The result is three classes: primary faces, connecting blends, and vertex blends where several faces meet. On the Kunos AE86 C-pillar the face we care about is one facet wide with no interior vertex, and that kills every method that classifies vertex neighbourhoods (slippage, Geomagic indicators, curvature k-means). What survives is face-based clustering plus a per-station cross-section analysis. At stations every 20-30 mm, the turn profile classifies the facets. Two tangent lines and a one-parameter tangent circle then place the boundaries: the 75 deg / 30 mm shoulder works out to R ≈ 23 mm with a setback of about 17.6 mm on each face. The facets themselves are chords that sag 0.5-1.2 mm inside that radius, so fitting must use vertices and vertex normals, never facet samples. That sag alone explains much of the current single-height-field miss (p95 2.4 mm). Build order is the Class-A one: fit the primaries on their own data and extend them under the shoulder, then build the shoulder from them. Our local FreeCAD 1.1.4 tests showed two working routes, a G2 section loft with zero curvature jump and an OCC fillet at 1e-4 deg G1. The foot fan closes last, as a setback n-sided `MakeFilling` patch, which reached 0.012 mm on a test hole. Nearly every numeric threshold for "blend vs face" below is our own starting value; no source publishes one.

## Every professional tool reduces segmentation to one cross-feature indicator

The only commercial pipeline whose algorithm can be traced is Geomagic's. The Várady/Facello/Terék patent models an object as large **primary surfaces joined by "connecting features" and vertex blends**. It segments with piecewise-linear indicator functions at the vertices. One is a "translational" indicator: a least-squares plane is fitted to about **20-30 neighbouring normals on the Gauss sphere**, and translation is strong when the first eigenvalue is small and the second large. The other is the principal-curvature ratio k1/k2; vertex-blend areas are where that ratio crosses a threshold. A watershed on the Morse complex, pruned by persistence with **a single "sensitivity" value**, picks the regions. The patent deliberately avoids global preset curvature thresholds. A blend is "thickened" from its smooth edge: the mid-curve sits at the cross-sectional maximum of the indicator and **the two boundaries at the adjacent cross-sectional minima** ([US8004517](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517)). Users of Design X see only that one knob. A practitioner runs Auto Segment at a low sensitivity of about 50-60 so regions do not fragment, then fixes under-segmented areas with Resegment or a hand-drawn Split ([CATI](https://www.cati.com/?p=126341)). Design X 2022 added a separate **"CAD Tessellated Mesh" segmentation option** ([release notes](https://download.laserscanning-europe.com/Geomagic/GeomagicDesignX_ReleaseNotes_v2022.0.0.pdf)). That is the nearest vendor admission that scan-tuned defaults misbehave on big-facet meshes like a game car.

The other vendors expose the same single scale in different units: PowerShape's "similarity angle" plus a matching tolerance ([Autodesk](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2022/ENU/PWRS-ReferenceHelp/files/GUID-17A3FEEA-751B-4FED-9BFC-5DC8BAAB7BE6.htm)), and Ansys's minimum fillet curvature angle with length limits, tied by **L = R·θ** ([Ansys](https://ansyshelp.ansys.com/public/Views/Secured/corp/v251/en/icm_help/igeom_featuredetectfillets.html)). Dassault's patented fillet test fits circles to a segment. It accepts a fillet only if the **mean circle-fit error and the radius variance are both small**, and takes the mean radius as the fillet radius ([Justia 20240135733](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20240135733)). No documented parameters were found for PolyWorks, Mesh2Surface, CATIA DSE or NX.

The academic methods split cleanly by their sampling unit. Slippage analysis needs patches of **m = 30 vertices**, and its authors warn that too small an m gives "essentially random" signatures. It does find smooth plane-cylinder joins, as separate one-slippable seams "which can be replaced by a surface blend" ([Gelfand & Guibas 2004](https://geometry.stanford.edu/lgl_2024/papers/gg-ssulsa-04/gg-ssulsa-04.pdf)). Lavoué et al. cluster vertices by curvature tensor and then merge regions with a term that compares each region's curvature with that of its *boundary* vertices. On badly tessellated CAD meshes, they insert a centre vertex into triangles whose three vertices are all "sharp" so that such faces get a sample of their own ([Lavoué et al. 2005](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/fdupont/publications/2005-CAD.pdf)). Face-based methods need no interior vertex, because a facet has exactly one normal. These are VSA with its L2,1 normal metric ([CGAL](https://doc.cgal.org/6.1/Surface_mesh_approximation/index.html)), Attene's bottom-up primitive merging ([CNR](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/52370)), and Wu & Kobbelt's hybrid VSA. Wu & Kobbelt's version adds sphere, cylinder and **rolling-ball blend proxies (spine c(t), radius r)** in a planes-first, then curved, then blends schedule. They fit blends by circle fits in windows of about 30 barycentres along a B-spline spine, and they call blend finding and fitting the method's weak point ([Wu & Kobbelt 2005](https://graphics.rwth-aachen.de/publication/87/hybrid1.pdf)).

## One facet across defeats vertex methods, so segment station by station

The pillar top has two vertex rows and nothing between them, so every top vertex is also a shoulder or window-edge vertex. Any per-vertex classifier gives the top face zero vertices of its own. A 30-vertex crawl on this 455-triangle, 1.1 m mesh would span roughly 150-300 mm, several times the pillar's whole cross-section. Gelfand's warning about random signatures applies in full. Face clustering survives but cannot represent the shoulder on its own: planar VSA either splits the 2-3 shoulder rows into extra planes or absorbs them into the top and side. The workable answer is to keep Wu & Kobbelt's blend model and replace their 30-barycentre windows with **per-station cross-sections**. That is also exactly the patent's "indicator across the feature", reduced to one dimension. The pillar is nearly translational along its length (a one-slippable band in Gelfand's terms), so the problem really is 1D at each station.

At each station, take the vertices in a slab about ±half a station spacing along the spine. Project them along the spine tangent into the station plane rather than intersecting the mesh: slice points lie on chords, while vertices lie on the designer surface. For a 15 mm half-slab under an along-pillar radius of about 1000 mm, the projection error is about 15²/2000 ≈ 0.1 mm (our estimate). The ordered vertices give a polyline from top to shoulder to side. At each vertex record the signed turn Δφ_k and the discrete curvature κ_k = Δφ_k / mean(adjacent segment lengths). Use the profile for classification. The blend centre is argmax κ. The blend runs between the points where cumulative turn reaches about **5 % and 95 %** of the total, with hysteresis: start at κ > 1/(3R), end below κ < 1/(10R). On a coarse mesh that profile is a step function. If three shoulder facets each subtend 25 deg and the tangent points sit at vertices, the vertex turns run 12.5, 25, 25, 12.5 deg. So the profile tells you *which facets* are shoulder, but not where the true tangent line lies.

Boundary *placement* is done by construction. Fit a line to the top-face vertices and one to the side-face vertices, excluding a guard band near the bend. Intersect them to get the virtual sharp corner X and the turn θ. A circle tangent to both lines has a single free parameter r. Its setback from X along each face is **d = r·tan(θ/2)** and its arc length is r·θ, so even 2-4 facets across the bend make this a well-posed fit (notes' derivation; the Ansys relation L = Rθ is the sourced form). For the AE86 numbers, θ = 75 deg = 1.309 rad over about 30 mm gives **r ≈ 23 mm and d ≈ 17.6 mm**. Cross-check r two ways: with L = Rθ, and with a linear normal-based centre fit p_i = c + R·n_i over the shoulder vertices. In 2D that is three unknowns and two equations per vertex, solved with `lstsq`; it is the coarse-mesh version of the osculating-sphere idea behind DeFillet ([DeFillet](https://xiaowuga.github.io/pub/DeFillet.html)). Our acceptance test, adapted from the Dassault and Wu-Kobbelt logic, is a **circle RMS ≤ 0.3-0.5 mm and a radius coefficient of variation ≤ 15-20 %** across stations; otherwise treat the blend as variable-radius.

Deciding "blend between two faces" against "one curved face" needs three tests, all unpublished as numbers. First, the turn must be concentrated: blend width below about half of each neighbour's width, and the neighbours' own κ below about a fifth of the blend peak. Second, the bend must be anisotropic and repeat station to station. Third, the union must fail a single-surface tolerance while each piece fits within 0.3-0.5 mm; the pillar already fails, at 2.4 mm. A cheap global check is the SVD of a station window's unit vertex normals. Normals that cluster mean a plane. Normals on a great circle (one small singular value) mean an extrusion or blend, and the null vector is the spine direction. Normals spread in 2D mean the translational blend has ended. Published normal-tensor thresholds give starting values: face if the normalised λ2 < 0.02, edge if λ2 > 0.1 and λ3 < 0.02, corner if λ3 > 0.1 ([Stony Brook PG 2011](https://www3.cs.stonybrook.edu/~qin/research/2011-pg-feature-classification.pdf); thresholds taken from a search summary, not re-read). Where k1/k2 falls toward 1 at the deck flare, the patent's vertex-blend logic takes over and the region becomes a fan, not a shoulder.

## Vertex normals carry the curvature that the facets sag away from

A facet across a curved region is a chord. For chord length L and end-normal angle θ, the sag is **s ≈ L·θ/8 = L²/(8R)**. To keep sag under a tolerance τ, a triangle may only be called flat if θ ≤ **θ_max = 8τ/L**. For τ = 0.3 mm that is about 3.1 deg on the 45 mm top facet but only 1.15 deg on a 120 mm body facet, so the project's current blanket 4 deg flat test is too loose on large facets. On the 23 mm shoulder, two facet rows (37.5 deg each) sag about **1.2 mm** and three rows (25 deg) about **0.55 mm**. Anything fitted to facet barycentres or dense facet samples therefore sits that far inside the true shoulder (elementary geometry; derivation in the notes). The PN-triangle quantity w_ij = (p_j − p_i)·n_i is the signed offset of p_j from p_i's tangent plane, and it is the natural per-edge classifier ([BFRA](https://cfuenfzig.lima-city.de/Publications/AComparisonOfLocalParametricC0BezierInterpolants_CG2010.pdf)). From it, κ_e ≈ (n_j − n_i)·e/L² and the predicted sag is |κ_e|L²/8. The signs of a_i = n_i·e/L and a_j = n_j·e/L give the edge type: opposite signs mean a convex or concave chord, the same sign means an inflection. A triangle counts as flat-intended only if all three edges are flat *and* its vertex normals agree with its own face normal. Three normals that agree with each other but are tilted off the face plane describe a tilted chord.

The authored normals themselves are shading devices. Blender's Weighted Normal modifier exists to "make some faces appear very flat" by letting large faces dominate the vertex normals, which pushes the curvature into the small faces ([Blender manual](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/4.3/modeling/modifiers/normals/weighted_normal.html)). The Kunos convention is undocumented. The cheap way to settle how much they are worth is to recompute area- and angle-weighted normals from the kn5 geometry. Stored normals within about 0.5 deg of the recomputed ones add nothing beyond the geometry; systematic differences mean they were authored and are worth more. Whatever the answer, a vertex on the top/shoulder seam carries an averaged normal that belongs to neither surface. Flag such vertices with the asymmetry test: on a true arc w_ij ≈ −w_ji, so treat |w_ij + w_ji| > 0.5·max(|w_ij|, |w_ji|) as biased. In the flat region, replace that normal with the face normal; in the blend, recompute it one-sided. Split normals (duplicated positions) mark the artist's own face boundaries and must never be averaged across. Degenerate normals are exactly where local interpolants lose G1 ([BFRA](https://cfuenfzig.lima-city.de/Publications/AComparisonOfLocalParametricC0BezierInterpolants_CG2010.pdf)).

When dense virtual samples are needed inside chord triangles, use **cubic PN triangles**. They reproduce the vertex tangent planes and stay within l/6 of the edges, they gave the best cross-edge normal continuity on real low-poly meshes, and they reduce exactly to the flat triangle when all normals equal the face normal. Avoid Phong tessellation at α = 3/4, which bulges outward and was the least accurate, and Nagata, which is the most accurate on clean spheres but blows up when an edge's two normals are near-parallel (ε had to be 0.01-0.06 on real meshes) ([BFRA](https://cfuenfzig.lima-city.de/Publications/AComparisonOfLocalParametricC0BezierInterpolants_CG2010.pdf)). Weight PN samples at 0.3-0.5 of a true vertex, and down-weight them on inflection edges and diagonal-split quads, where PN makes S-shaped undulations. Scaling BFRA's sphere table to R = 23 mm, PN's inward error at shoulder-like edge angles of 25-37 deg is on the order of 0.15-0.3 mm (our scaling). That is why the shoulder itself should not be fitted to PN data at all: it is *constructed* from the primaries, and its data only set r(s).

The normals enter the primary-face fits as linear Hermite rows. OCC's `GeomAPI_PointsToBSplineSurface` takes points only, with length/curvature/torsion smoothing weights ([OCCT](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_geom_a_p_i___points_to_b_spline_surface.html)), so solve in numpy and hand the poles to `Geom_BSplineSurface`. Fit each face as a height field in its own frame, with z along the region's mean normal. A data normal then gives exact slope rows f_x = −n_x/n_z and f_y = −n_y/n_z, each scaled by a length h of about half the facet edge to make it mm-equivalent; reject normals with n_z < cos 60 deg. Weight them at **w_n/w_p ≈ σ_p/(h·σ_n) ≈ 0.3** (σ_p 0.3 mm, h 60 mm, σ_n 1 deg; derived, not sourced). For fairness use bending energy rather than membrane energy, after Wang-Pottmann-Liu's α = 0, β = 0.001 on normalised curves ([WPL 2006](https://www.geometrie.tuwien.ac.at/geom/ig/papers/tr143.pdf)), starting at λ·L² ≈ 1e-3. Pick λ by holding out 10 % of the vertices. Weiss et al. confirm that smoothing weights cannot be set in one step from data alone ([SZTAKI](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/2966/)).

## Fit primaries first, build the 23 mm shoulder from them, close the foot last

Class-A and RE practice agree on the order. Build low-degree primary surfaces out to their theoretical intersections, then secondary surfaces, then blends and fillets, and trim late ([Alias](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2022/ENU/Alias-Tutorials/files/GUID-64611955-D2CC-44F2-98F0-D4F1FE931D8B.htm); [Novedge](https://novedge.com/blogs/design-news/rhino-3d-tip-class-a-surfacing-workflow-in-rhino)). RE likewise recovers blends from already-reconstructed primaries; Kós, Martin and Várady left variable-radius and vertex blends as later work ([SZTAKI 2459](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/2459/)). Commercial tools then "estimate radius from mesh" as averaged values along the edge ([CIMquest](https://cimquest-inc.com/?p=13124)). For the pillar this means fitting top and side **only to their own vertices, excluding a guard band of about one setback (15-18 mm) near the bend**. Fitting over the bend pulls the primaries toward the round and biases both θ and r. Extend each fit's domain 20-40 mm into the band. The one-facet top supports nothing richer than a ruled face across (degree 1) and a cubic with few spans along, which is exactly what the existing ruled-strip builder produces ([Narrow strip surfacing report](Narrow%20strip%20surfacing%20between%20panels.md)). A ruled top also extends linearly, which makes it its own tangent line for the virtual-corner check: the extended primaries must meet near the per-station X.

Two shoulder routes were tested headless in FreeCAD 1.1.4 / OCC 7.8.1, on a synthetic 1100 × 45 mm top and a 75 deg side with a 3 mm sinusoidal sag. **Route A** sews the extended, intersected primaries into a shell and calls `shell.makeFillet(r1, r2, [edge])`. Radii of 15→15, 15→2 and even 15→0 all returned valid shells, with a **maximum normal angle of 1e-4 deg** across the fillet boundaries. FreeCAD exposes only a linear radius law, and the result is G1 only, by construction ([exp1.py](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_cpillar/scripts/exp1.py), [exp2.py](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_cpillar/scripts/exp2.py)). OCC's tracker records fillets silently failing when the radius exceeds the available geometry ([OCC 31209](https://tracker.dev.opencascade.org/view.php?id=31209)). The r→0 success also contradicts the OCC guide's "law must be positive" caution ([OCCT guide](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/overview/html/occt_user_guides__modeling_algos.html)), so treat it as one observation, not a guarantee. **Route B** trims each primary at the setback d(s) and builds `Surface.BlendPoint`/`Surface.BlendCurve` sections from the face derivatives at 23 stations, then lofts them. G2 sections (degree-5 Bézier) gave **zero gap, zero normal angle and zero curvature jump**. G1 sections at size 0.5 left a 0.45 1/mm curvature jump. A relative tension of about 1.0 of the chord gave the most even curvature, with a near-constant 17-24 mm core. One trap: D1 must point from face 1 *into* the blend and onward into face 2; pointing both "toward the edge" kinks the sections ([exp3.py](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_cpillar/scripts/exp3.py)). Route B needs no intersection and accepts any width law, so it is the default; route A is the quick G1 fallback.

The foot fan follows the setback vertex blend of Várady and Rockwood. Stop each edge blend at a setback from the corner and fill the n-sided hole with a patch that is G1 to the surrounding ribbons ([index record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/5129645); [Salvi et al.](https://kevinpolisano.github.io/pdf-storage/M2-MSIAM/Modelisation_surfacique/MS_article.pdf)). ICEM Surf's Corner Blend is the commercial form ([ICEM spec](https://www.3ds.com/fileadmin/PRODUCTS-SERVICES/CATIA/PDF/ICEM_Surf_415_Advanced_Tools_Spec_Sheet_K76.pdf)), and Sipos-Várady-Salvi fit such patches to car-mesh regions at an average of 0.016 % of the bounding diagonal, but as implicit I-patches unusable in OCC ([arXiv 2204.11070](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.11070)). In OCC the tool is `BRepOffsetAPI_MakeFilling` with G1 edge constraints from the support faces ([OCCT BRepFill_Filling](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_fill___filling.html)). We refilled a 4-sided hole in a known smooth surface. At **degree 3, G1, it was off by 0.012 mm with a 1.4e-5 rad G1 error**; C0 constraints gave 0.083 mm. Degree 5 was *worse* (1.2 mm), and **order-2 "G2" constraints were silently treated as C0**. The patch is heavy (degree 8 × 8, 16-23 poles a side). In FreeCAD's binding, construct the filler empty and set parameters through `setResolParam`/`setConstrParam`/`setApproxParam` ([exp3.py](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_cpillar/scripts/exp3.py), [exp4.py](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_cpillar/scripts/exp4.py)). If the fan boundary must be G2, use a Gordon surface or a loft of G2 sections instead. Continuity checks need a hand-rolled checker, because FreeCAD does not bind OCC's `LocalAnalysis_SurfaceContinuity`, whose defaults are EpsG1 = 0.001 rad and Percent = 0.01 ([OCCT 7.8](https://dev.opencascade.org/doc/occt-7.8.0/refman/html/classLocalAnalysis__SurfaceContinuity.html)). The checker: compare normals at projected boundary samples for G1, and normal curvatures in the cross direction from `getDN` fundamental forms for G2 ([exp2.py](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_cpillar/scripts/exp2.py)).

## A twelve-step pipeline with starting parameters

The pipeline below is our synthesis. Sourced values are marked in the sections above; every other threshold is a starting value to tune on the AE86 data, with the 2.4 mm p95 single-height-field fit as the baseline to beat.

| # | Step | Starting parameters | Pass test |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Load the pillar region; find split normals (duplicate positions); recompute area/angle normals and compare with stored normals | Agreement threshold 0.5 deg | Decides the weight w_n (low if normals merely restate the geometry) |
| 2 | Coarse face clustering over the panel: sharp edges first, then VSA-L2,1 / Attene-style facet merging | Dihedral > 20 deg = crease | Top, side, deck and quarter appear as candidate regions |
| 3 | Spine = PCA axis or existing feature curve; stations along it; slab vertices projected along the spine tangent | Station spacing 20-30 mm; slab ±½ spacing | Every station has at least 6-10 vertices |
| 4 | Turn profile per station (code below): classify segments as top / shoulder / side | Cumulative turn 5 % / 95 %; hysteresis start κ > 1/(3R), end κ < 1/(10R) | Same class pattern on neighbouring stations |
| 5 | Gauss-sphere SVD of the station-window normals | Normalised λ2 < 0.02 face; λ2 > 0.1 & λ3 < 0.02 blend; λ3 > 0.1 fan | Fan stations flagged where k1/k2 → 1 |
| 6 | Per station: lines through top and side vertices (guard band excluded), corner X, θ, one-parameter tangent circle r, setback d = r·tan(θ/2); cross-check with the (c, R) normal fit and r = arc/θ | Guard band ≈ 1 setback (15-18 mm) | Circle RMS ≤ 0.3-0.5 mm; the three r estimates within 15 % |
| 7 | Along the spine: smooth θ(s), r(s), d(s); find vanishing station s0 (θ → 0 or d < facet size); choose blend stop s1 | 4-6 B-spline control values over 1.1 m; constant r if CV ≤ 15-20 %; s1 = s0 − 1-2 blend widths | No zebra ripple from r(s) |
| 8 | Classify facets per edge: flat vs chord, inflection, biased seam normals; replace or one-side seam normals | θ_max = 8τ/L, τ = 0.3 mm; asymmetry ratio 0.5 | Top facet flat-intended, shoulder facets chords |
| 9 | Fit primaries as height fields in their own frames: position rows, Hermite slope rows, bending fairness; top ruled across × cubic along | n_z ≥ cos 60 deg; w_n/w_p ≈ 0.3; λL² = 1e-3, swept 1e-5…1e-1 by 10 % hold-out; knot spans ≥ vertex spacing; domain +20-40 mm | Held-out vertex p95 ≤ 0.3-0.5 mm; extended faces meet near every X |
| 10 | Shoulder, route B: trim lines at d(s); G2 `BlendCurve` sections from `getDN(u,v,0,1/2)`; loft; retrim primaries to the loft's boundary isos (route A fillet as G1 fallback) | 25-45 stations; tension 0.8-1.0; degree 5 for G2 | Shoulder vertices within 0.3-0.5 mm; G1 ≤ 0.1 deg, Δκ ≈ 0 |
| 11 | Foot fan: n-sided hole bounded by the blend end section, top, side, deck and quarter edges; `MakeFilling` with G1 edges plus a few interior mesh points | `setResolParam(3, 20-30, 3-4, False)`, `setConstrParam(1e-5, 0.01-0.05, ≤0.01, 0.1)`, `setApproxParam(8, 9)`, `add(edge, face, 1, True)`; 10-30 interior points away from the edges | `G1Error()` ≤ 0.0017 rad; deviation from the fan vertices ≤ 0.5 mm |
| 12 | Verify all seams and render zebra | G0 ≤ 0.01-0.05 mm; G1 ≤ 0.1 deg; G2 gap ≤ 1-5 % (or Δκ ≤ 0.001 1/mm on near-flats) | All vertices p95 well under the 2.4 mm baseline |

The station maths for steps 4, 6 and 8 is a few lines of numpy. Convention: 2D station coordinates, polyline ordered from top to side, outward normals, convex bend.

```python
import numpy as np

def turn_profile(q):                      # q: (m,2) station vertices, top -> side
    d = np.diff(q, axis=0); l = np.linalg.norm(d, axis=1); d /= l[:, None]
    turn = np.arctan2(d[:-1, 0]*d[1:, 1] - d[:-1, 1]*d[1:, 0], (d[:-1]*d[1:]).sum(1))
    kappa = turn / (0.5*(l[:-1] + l[1:]))  # per interior vertex
    cum = np.cumsum(np.abs(turn)) / np.abs(turn).sum()
    return turn, kappa, cum               # blend = vertices with 0.05 < cum < 0.95

def centre_radius_from_normals(p, n):     # p_i = c + R n_i, lstsq in (cx, cy, R)
    n = n / np.linalg.norm(n, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    A = np.zeros((2*len(p), 3)); A[0::2, 0] = 1; A[1::2, 1] = 1
    A[0::2, 2], A[1::2, 2] = n[:, 0], n[:, 1]
    (cx, cy, R), *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, p.reshape(-1), rcond=None)
    return np.array([cx, cy]), R

def tangent_circle(X, u1, u2, pts, r_grid):   # u1,u2: unit dirs from X along top / side
    bis = (u1 + u2) / np.linalg.norm(u1 + u2)
    half = 0.5*np.arccos(np.clip(u1 @ u2, -1, 1))       # half the interior angle
    rms = [np.sqrt(np.mean((np.linalg.norm(pts - (X + bis*r/np.sin(half)), axis=1) - r)**2))
           for r in r_grid]
    k = int(np.argmin(rms)); r = r_grid[k]
    return r, rms[k], r/np.tan(half)                    # radius, fit RMS, setback d

def edge_sag(pi, pj, ni, nj):             # 3D, per mesh edge
    e = pj - pi; L = np.linalg.norm(e)
    kappa = (nj - ni) @ e / L**2          # = 1/R on a true arc
    return abs(kappa)*L**2/8, ni @ e/L, nj @ e/L   # sag, a_i, a_j (a_i<0<a_j convex)
```

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. "How do we segment a smooth region?" becomes "at what scale do we measure the turn?" Every professional tool answers with one scale knob, and on a mesh whose faces are a single facet wide the only honest scale is the cross-section station. That makes segmentation and radius recovery the same computation: the turn profile says which facets belong to the bend, and the tangent-line construction says where the bend really starts. Because of that, the shoulder never needs fitting to data. It is built from the primaries, and the mesh only supplies r(s) and validates the result. This also explains why earlier single-surface attempts failed: they tried to fit chords that sag up to 1.2 mm below the shoulder they stand for.

The open risks are concrete. No source gives the blend-vs-face, hysteresis or acceptance numbers, so they need one tuning pass against the AE86 pillar's known top/shoulder/side split. The Kós-Martin-Várady comparison of radius estimators, Benkő-Várady's smooth-region segmentation and the Geomagic GMP 2006 paper could not be read. Kunos's normal convention is unknown until step 1 measures it. `MakeFilling` has only been tested on a 4-sided hole, and its G2 option does not work in this build, so a 3- or 5-sided foot fan is the step most likely to need a fallback: Gordon or G2 section patches split into 4-sided pieces. Developable-chart segmentation (D-Charts) was not researched and is a credible alternative for the ruled top and side faces.
