# Build the strip from the curves, never after

Professional surfacing tools and the reverse-engineering literature agree on one rule: **a narrow strip such as a step, flange, return or lamp-surround ledge is generated from one fair driving curve, and the wide neighbours are trimmed on the very edges that the same operation produces.** Nobody fits the strip separately and then hopes it meets the panels. Autodesk Alias's Panel Gap and Fillet Flange build a G1/G2 fillet off the panel plus a **ruled (degree-1) flange wall**, and they trim the panel automatically on the fillet edge they just computed. The Várady/Geomagic school fits the primary panels first, traces a "feature skeleton" on the mesh, and "thickens" each strip into two boundary trimlines that both the strip and the panels use. For an 8 mm × 260 mm strip (aspect ratio about 1:32) this means three things. Fit the boundary curve or curves once, from the mesh, as fair cubic B-splines. Lay them on each panel as 2D pcurves. Build the strip directly on those edges: ruled for a flat wall or chamfer, a loft or fillet with tangent conditions where the strip is curved across its width or visible. In FreeCAD/OpenCascade this route is exact by construction, because `Part.makeRuledSurface` (BRepFill::Face) reuses the caller's edge objects. The resulting panel/strip/panel shell has nothing to sew, and its edge tolerances stay near 1e-7. No public OEM tolerance standard exists. Vendor guidance puts G0 at 0.01 to 0.001 mm and visible tangent breaks below 0.1°. The weakest part of the evidence is automatic detection of strips on sparse, low-poly meshes: the literature gives the method (cross-sectional minima of a curvature-type indicator) but no numeric thresholds.

## Alias builds a step as fillet + ruled wall + trim in one command

The best-documented industrial tool is Alias's **Panel Gap**. It creates "the whole set of surfaces for a finished gap" along a chain of tangent-continuous curves-on-surface: primary and secondary flanges, the fillets between panel and flange, and an optional close-out across the bottom ([Alias Panel Gap](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2015/ENU/Alias-Reference/files/SurfacesRollededgePanelgap.htm)). The construction choices are worth copying literally. **Flanges are ruled in V (degree 1)**, and the close-out is also ruled. The fillet to the panel is **G1 by default, optionally G2**, and sized by a centre radius or a tangent offset against an imaginary "wall". Gap width is a "gap distance" between the two rolled edges, which can vary along the length through manipulators. Gap Position can be "Center line" (gap centred on the input curve) or "Primary side" (one edge on the curve, the other offset). Flange direction comes from a sweep angle (default 90°) or from a pull/parting-line vector with draft. Most importantly, **Trim Type "Automatic" trims the panel to the fillet edges** that the command computed, and "Curves-on-surface" just lays those edges on the panel. The panel boundary and the strip boundary are therefore the same computed curve ([Alias Panel Gap](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2015/ENU/Alias-Reference/files/SurfacesRollededgePanelgap.htm)).

**Fillet Flange** is the single-panel version, which suits a shut-line return or a lamp-surround lip. It is a G1/G2 fillet along an edge, curve-on-surface or isoparm, followed by a "linear extension" flange whose **V degree is always 1**, with Auto Trim on the base surface ([Alias Fillet Flange](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2015/ENU/Alias-Reference/files/SurfacesRollededgeFilletflange.htm)). Multi-Surface Draft builds a flange along a chain of edges, on one or both sides of a curve ([Alias Flange Surfaces overview](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2014/ENU/Alias/files/GUID-462B9CDD-B8ED-4317-87B1-547F9281588C.htm)). Alias's Class A guidance treats "split lines, flanges, and panel gaps" and "smaller and detail fillets" as their own tolerance categories, separate from the main A-surfaces ([Alias Understanding Class A](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2024/ENU/Alias-Getting-Started/files/theory-builders/GUID-64611955-D2CC-44F2-98F0-D4F1FE931D8B.html)). Industry accepts that narrow strips are construction geometry built to rules, not freeform sculpted surfaces.

Rhino has no dedicated step tool. Its documentation recommends Sweep2 "when you want to control the location of the edges of the surface", using the edges of other surfaces as rails ([Rhino guide](https://www.rhino3d.com/new-source/docs/guides/modeling/rhino-basic-curves-surfaces)). McNeel staff note that the RhinoCommon `Brep.CreateBlendSurface` and the BlendSrf command share little more than a name. For blends whose edges meet at the ends, they suggest `Brep.CreateFromSweep` with shape curves instead ([McNeel Discourse](https://discourse.mcneel.com/t/sweep2-rails-edge-continuity-and-or-blendsrf-component/110972)). The usual Rhino practitioner sequence (unsourced, but consistent with the above) is: pull the feature curve onto the panel, OffsetCrvOnSrf to get the second edge, split the panel with both curves, then Sweep2 or BlendSrf between the trimmed edges and check with EdgeContinuity/Zebra. Geomagic Design X handles car bodies by "hybrid modelling": NURBS fitted to auto-segmented mesh regions for the organic panels, and feature modelling for prismatic details. Its whole-model auto-surfacing gives a patchwork "dumb solid" that is poor for editing ([Hawk Ridge](https://hawkridgesys.com/blog/hybrid-modeling-with-geomagic-design-x)). Public ICEM Surf and CATIA ICEM Shape Design documentation for flange or step tools could not be found, so their exact commands are an open gap. The shared pattern in the documented tools is clear enough to act on.

## Reverse engineering fits the panels first and derives the strip

The academic pipeline from Várady, Martin, Benkő, Kós and colleagues models a CAD-like part as **primary surfaces**, **connecting features** (fillets, chamfers: long four-sided strips) and **vertex blends** where strips meet. Benkő, Martin and Várady list "adding blends last" as one of the four core steps of B-rep reverse engineering ([SZTAKI eprint](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/2654/)). Langbein et al. go further: they detect rolling-ball blends in the data but store them only as edge attributes, and they build the blend surfaces after the primaries have been regularised ([Langbein et al., SM 2004](https://diglib7.eg.org/bitstream/handle/10.2312/sm20041398/255-260_langbein.pdf)). Kós, Martin and Várady show that once the primaries are known **and a radius is estimated, the whole constant-radius blend can be computed** ([SZTAKI eprint](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/2459/)). Its two contact curves then lie exactly on both primaries. Andor et al. fit several curves and surfaces at once with constraints between them (tangency, parallelism and so on), and resolve conflicting constraints numerically ([SZTAKI](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/2967/)).

The Geomagic patent US8004517B1 (Edelsbrunner, Facello, Gloth, Terék, Várady) describes the industrial form of this pipeline in the most detail. It runs Morse segmentation on per-vertex indicator functions (curvature, slippability, sweepability, a "translational" indicator built from the ratio of principal curvatures), then grows "separator" triangle strips. A feature skeleton is traced through a translational vector field estimated from about **20 to 30 points per vertex**. The patent then **thickens each smooth feature edge into two longitudinal boundary curves (trimlines)**, with profile curves **tangential to the adjacent primary regions** ([US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)). The strip is represented as a "double strip of quadrangles" or as a rolling-ball blend. The vertex blends at the strip ends are n-sided or setback patches constrained to their neighbours. The patent gives three topologies for strip ends: setback vertex blends, n-sided vertex blends with no spring curves, or strips with end pieces and no vertex blends ([US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)).

This school offers two ways to get the shared edge. **Derive it from the surfaces**: intersect two fitted primaries for a sharp crease (as Point2CAD does, recovering edges by intersecting adjacent fitted surfaces ([Point2CAD](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04962))), or compute rolling-ball contact curves for a blend. Or **fit the curve first** from the traced boundary and constrain the surfaces to it. Lai, Hu and Pottmann note that a sharp feature in a B-spline surface **must be a parameter line**, so the parametrisation is aligned with features before fitting ([abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/surface-fitting-based-on-a-feature-sensitive-parametrization/811924506372210692-2539)). For an 8 mm step between two near-parallel panels, the intersection route is the wrong choice: intersecting nearly tangent surfaces is ill-conditioned, and Split-and-Fit reports B-spline fitting in Point2CAD-style pipelines as "unstable and occasionally yields implausible results" ([Split-and-Fit](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.05261)). Fitting the boundary curve from the data is the robust option.

For the curve fit itself, Wang, Pottmann and Liu compare three methods. Point-distance minimisation (alternate foot-point projection and least squares) converges only linearly. Tangent-distance minimisation is **unstable where curvature varies strongly**. Squared distance minimisation (SDM), a quasi-Newton method with a curvature-based quadratic error model, converges faster and more stably ([Wang, Pottmann & Liu, TOG 2006](https://www.geometrie.tuwien.ac.at/geom/ig/publications/oldpub/2006/wpl_curves_06/wpl_curves_06.html)). For a 260 mm boundary, the standard recipe is: order the traced polyline, give it chord-length or centripetal parameters, fit a cubic with about 6 to 10 control points plus a bending penalty, and iterate foot points. Add knots only where the residual exceeds tolerance. That recipe is textbook B-spline practice rather than something a cited paper states for strips. No paper found fits one 3D B-spline to a mesh crease and then trims two independently fitted panels on it with stated tolerances. The workflow below is assembled from the pieces.

## Ruled walls for flat strips, tangent fillets where light falls

Which surface type a strip should be depends on its cross-section, not its width. The table below combines the industrial defaults with the geometric constraints.

| Strip cross-section | Surface type | Continuity to neighbours | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight wall, flange, chamfer, flat ledge | Ruled (degree 1 across width) | G0 on both rails by construction | Alias flanges and close-outs are degree 1 ([Panel Gap](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2015/ENU/Alias-Reference/files/SurfacesRollededgePanelgap.htm)) |
| Visible rolled edge between panel and wall | Fillet / rolling ball, or cubic loft with tangent rows | G1 default, G2 for Class A | Alias Fillet Flange ([source](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2015/ENU/Alias-Reference/files/SurfacesRollededgeFilletflange.htm)); Kós et al. ([source](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/2459/)) |
| Crowned or curved across width | 3- or 4-row B-spline whose outer pole rows are the rail curves | G0 exact; G1 via inner rows | Patent profile curves tangential to primaries ([US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)) |
| Step (normal turns twice) | Fillet + ruled wall + fillet | G1/G2 at each fillet | Panel Gap primary and secondary rolled edges ([source](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2015/ENU/Alias-Reference/files/SurfacesRollededgePanelgap.htm)) |

A ruled strip between two trimmed panel edges is G0 on both long edges automatically, because its rails *are* those edges. It is only G1 where the panels happen to be tangent to the ruling direction. That is why professional tools put fillets in front of the ruled wall instead of relying on a bare crease. Where the real part has a sharp pressed edge, a crease is honest. Elsewhere it shows as a highlight break. No public source gives typical fillet radii for an 8 mm step. That needs OEM standards or training material.

The less obvious problem with ruled strips is **parameter pairing**. Different correspondences between the same two rails give different ruled surfaces. Wang and Tang find the best bridging map as a **shortest path in a graph** over both discretised rails, with weightable objectives including minimal total twist and minimal normal variation. A ruling is twist-free when the surface normals along it are parallel ([Wang & Tang](https://mewangcl.github.io/pubs/JCISEOptmTrgl.pdf)). Chen, Tang and Joneja then move points to raise developability further ([abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/design-of-developable-interpolating-strips/813275659949309952-19239)). This matters in practice because OpenCascade's `GeomFill_Profiler` pairs rails by **linearly normalised parameter**: each curve's knots are remapped onto the widest range, degrees are raised, and knots are merged. Where knot counts still differ, it averages interior knots by index ([OCCT GeomFill_Profiler.cxx](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Open-Cascade-SAS/OCCT/V7_8_0/src/GeomFill/GeomFill_Profiler.cxx)). Two rails interpolated over different point distributions will therefore twist even when they are geometrically parallel. The simplest fix keeps edges exact: **generate both rails from one common station parameter**, with the same `Parameters=` list for both interpolations. A Wang–Tang dynamic programme with monotone pairing (which stops rulings crossing) is the general fallback for rails that already exist.

## Tolerances are company-specific, but the numbers cluster

No universal Class A standard exists. Alias states that "there are no absolute rules for tolerances or continuity levels" and that automotive companies keep their own ([Alias Understanding Class A](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2024/ENU/Alias-Getting-Started/files/theory-builders/GUID-64611955-D2CC-44F2-98F0-D4F1FE931D8B.html)). The published figures still cluster tightly. Alias gives a **typical maximum G0 gap of 0.01 to 0.001 mm** for prototyping and production, and says a tangent break **below 0.1°** is normally invisible. It measures G2 as curvature deviation (R1 − R2)/(R1 + R2), without a threshold ([Alias Construction Tolerances](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2024/ENU/Alias-Getting-Started/files/theory-builders/GUID-2FCE06EB-8EF7-4507-92F7-82A73A0DF378.html)). CATIA counts faces as tangent-continuous below **0.5°** ([IBM support](https://www.ibm.com/support/pages/node/5488983)). That is a software threshold, not a quality target. A French proposal for Class B gives G0 0.001 mm and G1 0.2° ([Wikipedia: Class A surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_A_surface)), though copies of the figure disagree. Alias's Panel Gap also has a built-in "Gap check" that colours quills by a threshold ([Panel Gap](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2015/ENU/Alias-Reference/files/SurfacesRollededgePanelgap.htm)).

For a replica destined for a game mesh, these CAD tolerances are orders of magnitude tighter than anything visible after tessellation. The useful working targets are: **G0 essentially zero** (shared topology, about 1e-7 in OCCT); **G1 ≤ 0.1° on visible edges** such as fillet-to-panel; and G2 judged by eye with zebra or curvature combs, not by number. The tolerance that actually bites in this pipeline is fit deviation to the source mesh, which the existing panel regression already measures. One geometric guard comes from the healing tools. OCCT has a dedicated `ShapeFix_FixSmallFace.fixStripFace`, because thin strip faces are a known failure case ([FreeCAD Part.pyd docstrings](file:///D:/FreeCAD/lib/Part.pyd)). A reasonable rule (an inference, not a sourced standard) is to keep strip width at least 100 times the largest edge tolerance. An 8 mm strip with exact shared edges clears that easily. One built from sewn booleans at 0.1 mm tolerance does not.

## Detecting strips means finding two cross-sectional minima

The proven detection approach is cross-sectional rather than per-edge. In the Geomagic pipeline, a separator strip is grown around a high-curvature or translational ridge until "the translational strength falls below a certain threshold". The **strip's two boundaries are placed at the two adjacent cross-sectional minima** of a similarity indicator, with the midline at the cross-sectional maximum. Tracing starts in the middle of the feature (the most stable point) and runs outwards in both directions, with a stabilising term against oscillation ([US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)). Strip width falls out as the distance between the boundaries. Setbacks at strip ends are taken from the maximum width of neighbouring features. Where a strip is narrow enough that a boundary vanishes, the end blends merge, and self-intersecting thickened boundaries are pushed apart with a weighted Voronoi diagram ([US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)). In effect, the strip's medial axis becomes its midline.

Sparse game meshes with long thin triangles are where naive tests fail. Lai, Zhou, Hu and Martin segment CAD meshes using **only normals, not dihedral angles**, because dihedral angles are noise-sensitive ([Lai et al., SPM 2006](https://mysite.cs.cf.ac.uk/Yukun.Lai/papers/fsseg.pdf)). Lai, Zhou, Hu, Wallner and Pottmann detect features at multiple scales with **integral invariants** over neighbourhoods, after feature-sensitive isotropic remeshing, and treat a sharp edge as the limit of a blend whose radius tends to zero ([TU Wien](https://www.geometrie.tuwien.ac.at/ig/publications/oldpub/2007/lzhwp_robust_07/lzhwp_robust_07.html)). Benkő and Várady note that segmentation is "relatively simple if regions are bounded by sharp edges and small blends". The hard case is smoothly connected regions ([abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/segmentation-methods-for-smooth-point-regions-of-conventional-engineering-objects/812389137507155969-2539)). Learning-based reconstruction (ComplexGen, Point2CAD) offers nothing specific for thin faces, and it is documented as sensitive to sampling and inconsistent in topology ([ComplexGen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.14573); [Split-and-Fit](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.05261)). It is the wrong tool for these strips.

Combined into a detector for a kn5-style mesh: remesh or densely resample first. Estimate normals over a ball of fixed radius (a few millimetres) rather than over the one-ring. Take cross-section planes every few millimetres along a candidate feature, and in each plane plot normal angle against arc length. A step shows **two turns of the normal in opposite senses** separated by a flat run. A fillet or chamfer shows **one monotone turn**. Strip edges sit where the normal-change rate falls back to the panel's background level. Classify the region as a narrow connecting strip when its width w(s) stays far below its length L. The 8 mm × 260 mm target sits at w/L ≈ 0.03. **No paper read gives a numeric width or aspect-ratio threshold**, so any cutoff (for example w/L < 0.1 and w < 15 mm) has to be calibrated against the replica's own known strips. The best ground truth is to digitise them once by eye from the reference photos.

## FreeCAD/OCCT can make shared edges exact by construction

The implementation finding that matters most comes from the OCCT source: **`BRepFill::Face` reuses the input edges and vertices as-is**. Only the two short end edges are new. It adds exact straight-line pcurves (`Geom2d_Line` at v = 0 and v = 1) for the strip onto the caller's edges, then calls `BRepLib::SameParameter` at `Precision::Confusion()` (1e-7) ([OCCT BRepFill.cxx](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Open-Cascade-SAS/OCCT/V7_8_0/src/BRepFill/BRepFill.cxx)). FreeCAD's `Part.makeRuledSurface(e1, e2, orientation)` is a thin wrapper that calls `BRepFill::Face` for edges and `BRepFill::Shell` for wires ([FreeCAD TopoShapeExpansion.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/main/src/Mod/Part/App/TopoShapeExpansion.cpp)). Any edge object passed in therefore becomes a topologically shared edge. `GeomFill_Generator` makes the strip an exact B-spline with V degree 1 ([OCCT GeomFill_Generator.cxx](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Open-Cascade-SAS/OCCT/V7_8_0/src/GeomFill/GeomFill_Generator.cxx)). The automatic orientation compares cross-products at 10 % and 90 % along the curves and reverses the second curve if they disagree ([TopoShapeExpansion.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/main/src/Mod/Part/App/TopoShapeExpansion.cpp)). Once the rails are oriented explicitly, pass `orientation=1` so a degenerate case cannot silently flip into a bow-tie. Lofts, `makeFilledFace` and Coons/`GeomFill_BSplineCurves` fills all build new boundary geometry. `GeomFill_BSplineCurves` also rejects two non-contiguous curves outright ([OCCT reference](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_geom_fill___b_spline_curves.html)).

The way to put the same edge on the panels is the pcurve pattern used throughout CurvesWB. Express the boundary as a `Geom2d` curve in the panel's (u,v) space, call `c2d.toShape(panelSurface, first, last)` to get an edge that lies exactly on the panel, then build the trimmed panel with `Part.Face(surface, wire)` followed by `validate()`. CurvesWB's `validated_face` retries by doubling the wire tolerance up to 1e-4 ([CurvesWB Sketch_On_Surface.py](https://github.com/tomate44/CurvesWB/blob/master/freecad/Curves/Sketch_On_Surface.py)). CurvesWB's own blend tool assembles the same `Part.Shell([f1, bsface, f2])` panel/strip/panel triple ([blend_curve.py](https://github.com/tomate44/CurvesWB/blob/master/freecad/Curves/blend_curve.py)). Boolean trims (`common`, `cut`, `BOPTools.SplitAPI.slice` as in CurvesWB TrimFace) work, but they produce **new intersection edges**. Those edges then have to be sewn to the strip, and sewn tolerances grow with whatever the input faces carried ([TrimFace.py](https://github.com/tomate44/CurvesWB/blob/master/freecad/Curves/TrimFace.py); [OCCT forum](https://dev.opencascade.org/content/sewing-two-faces-results-very-high-tolerance)). The recommended sequence, with the station trick that keeps rulings untwisted:

```python
# ts: common station parameters along the strip; uvA/uvB: boundary points in each panel's (u,v)
cA = Part.Geom2d.BSplineCurve2d(); cA.interpolate(Points=uvA, Parameters=ts)
cB = Part.Geom2d.BSplineCurve2d(); cB.interpolate(Points=uvB, Parameters=ts)
eA = cA.toShape(panelA.Surface, ts[0], ts[-1])        # exact on panel A
eB = cB.toShape(panelB.Surface, ts[0], ts[-1])        # exact on panel B
strip = Part.makeRuledSurface(eA, eB, 1)              # reuses eA, eB (BRepFill::Face)
fA = Part.Face(panelA.Surface, Part.Wire([eA] + otherA)); fA.validate()
fB = Part.Face(panelB.Surface, Part.Wire([eB] + otherB)); fB.validate()
sh = Part.Shell([fA, strip.Faces[0], fB])
assert sh.getTolerance(1, Part.Edge) < 1e-5 and not sh.check(True)
```

The acceptance test in the last line can be extended with `sh.getFreeEdges()` (only the outer boundary should remain), point sampling with `distToShape` along each shared edge, a ruling-angle check (each `S(t,1) − S(t,0)` roughly perpendicular to both rails), and the mesh-deviation check from the existing panel regression ([FreeCAD Part.pyd docstrings](file:///D:/FreeCAD/lib/Part.pyd)). Where a strip must curve across its width, replace `makeRuledSurface` with `BSplineSurface.buildFromPolesMultsKnots` on a 3- or 4-row pole grid. Its outer rows are the degree- and knot-matched rail poles (CurvesWB's `match_degrees`/`normalize_knots`/`match_knots`, then `U_linear_surface` generalised to more rows ([curves_to_surface.py](https://github.com/tomate44/CurvesWB/blob/master/freecad/Curves/curves_to_surface.py))). The inner rows set the tangent directions towards each panel. The edges are then iso-curves of the strip and can still be reused. CurvesWB's HQ Ruled Surface removes twist by **approximating new rail curves** ([HQRuledSurfaceFP.py](https://github.com/tomate44/CurvesWB/blob/master/freecad/Curves/HQRuledSurfaceFP.py)). If it is used, the new curves must become the trim curves for the panels too. Trimming panels on the old curves while the strip uses the new ones reopens the gap.

Three things remain unverified. Nobody has yet confirmed that `Part.Face(surface, wire)` keeps the given edges rather than copying them. Nor that `BRepFill::Shell` reuses edges the way `BRepFill::Face` does. Nor that a pcurve's parameter survives `toShape` exactly enough for station-to-station rulings. Each needs a five-minute test (compare `edge.isSame()` across the faces of the shell) before the builder relies on it. Two more practical guards: keep panel surfaces slightly larger than their trimmed area so pcurves stay strictly inside the domain, and avoid rails whose ends coincide, because `BRepFill::Face` then creates a degenerate end edge ([BRepFill.cxx](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Open-Cascade-SAS/OCCT/V7_8_0/src/BRepFill/BRepFill.cxx)).

## Conclusion

The "thin-strip builder" is not a fitting problem like the panels. It is a **topology problem with a fitting step at the front**. The panels should be fitted to the mesh. The strip should be fitted only in the sense that its one or two driving curves come from the mesh, and everything after that is built, not fitted. That reverses the natural instinct to fit an 8 mm surface to the few triangles that represent it. Those triangles are too sparse to constrain a surface, but a 260 mm boundary curve traced across many cross-sections is well constrained. The main risk moves from surface quality to curve quality: a kink or wobble in the driving curve turns into a kink in the strip and in both panel edges. That is why Alias insists on G1 input chains, and why the curve fit (SDM, few control points, a fairing penalty) deserves the effort the panels got.

A second implication: one curve can serve both strip types. A ruled flat step and a G1 rolled edge differ only in what is built between the same two pcurves, so the builder can start ruled (exact, cheap, verifiable) and upgrade individual strips to multi-row tangent strips where the photos show a soft highlight. Detection is the least mature part of the evidence. Until a calibrated cross-section detector exists, marking strips by eye on the reference photos and letting the code trace and fit them is the more reliable path, and it fits the project's existing judge-by-eye method.
