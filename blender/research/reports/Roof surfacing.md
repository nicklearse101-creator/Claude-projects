# Make the roof the master surface

Professional Class-A and reverse-engineering practice builds the roof **first**, as one large, simple, oversized primary surface. Every neighbour then takes its edge, tangent and curvature *from* the roof: the drip-rail strip, the A- and C-pillar tops, and the windscreen and hatch headers. The roof is trimmed late, on curves those neighbours share. Nothing is fitted separately and expected to meet afterwards. For the Kunos AE86 this means one full-width, mirror-symmetric B-spline face of low degree with few poles. The data for that face matters as much as its shape. The mesh's **150 mm triangles are chords that sit 0.6-1.3 mm inside the true roof** (2.6 mm at worst), so fitting to facet samples biases the crown downward, and down-weighting them only shrinks the bias. The fit has to use **vertices only**. Two optional additions help: Phong samples at **α = ½** (not the visual default ¾), and normal rows weighted by their measured accuracy. The usual thin-plate fairing also flattens a crown, by **0.05-0.7 mm** at the weights where it starts to smooth noise. A third-derivative (curvature-variation) energy removes noise without that bias. In FreeCAD 1.1.4 / OCCT 7.8.1, our local tests found a working chain. Our own numpy least squares fits the scattered vertices, with optional G1/G2 rows (**dev 0.015 mm, G2 curvature jump 0.4 %**). `Part.Face(srf, Part.Wire(sorted 3D edges)) + validate()` is the only trim that later sewed. `sewShape(0.01)` then gives one watertight shell. `MakeFilling` reaches G1 but not G2, because its "order 2" is silently treated as C0. Public sources give no roof-specific patch count or scan tolerance. Every acceptance number below that is not an Alias figure is our own.

## Class-A builds one oversized primary and accelerates its crown

The Class-A canon puts surface layout first. Autodesk calls "the layout of the surface data" the key to Class A and lists Bézier (single-span) surfaces among its golden rules, for internal smoothness ([Autodesk Alias](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2022/ENU/Alias-Tutorials/files/GUID-64611955-D2CC-44F2-98F0-D4F1FE931D8B.htm)). A single-span NURBS is exactly a Bézier patch, and ICEM's order is Alias's degree + 1 ([Alias single-span](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2014/ENU/Alias/files/GUID-4D7B7604-C5C3-4B94-ABDC-741DA54B38E9.htm)). Rhino's industrial-design guide is the most concrete public rulebook. It recommends **single-span curves of degree 2 or 5** and single-span surfaces wherever possible. It treats the surfaces as a primary → secondary → tertiary tree whose quality is set by the primaries. It warns against Rebuild, NetworkSrf/Patch and three-sided patches, and against assuming G2 across a mirror plane ([Rhino surface modeling tips](https://www.rhino3d.com/docs/guides/industrial-design/surface-modeling-tips/)). Practitioners cap the degree at about 7 and prefer 5 ([McNeel forum](https://discourse.mcneel.com/t/class-a-surfaces-in-rhino/5583?page=2)). Reverse-engineering software defaults point the same way. Siemens NX's `FitSurfaceBuilder`, which fits surfaces to facet bodies, defaults to **degree 5 × 5 with one patch** in each direction ([NX Open](https://docs.plm.automation.siemens.com/data_services/resources/nx/12/nx_api/custom/en_US/nxopen_python_ref/NXOpen.Features.FitSurfaceBuilder.html)). LS-PrePost instead adds control points until a "Max Tol" is met and then reports the grid it chose ([Ansys](https://ansyshelp.ansys.com/public/Views/Secured/corp/v242/en/lsdyna_prepost_tut/ch08s01.html)). In short, the industry starts with few poles and raises the degree or count only until a deviation tolerance is met.

The roof-specific advice is thin but consistent. LearningAlias's roof module starts with the roof, checks every curve against curvature combs, and insists the roof be **"accelerated" toward the cant rail**. Its curvature should rise toward the edge so that the transition surface does not have to "perform miracles" ([LearningAlias](https://learningalias.co.uk/showmod.php?m=128&t=65)). Autodesk's Alias Automotive series has dedicated "Roof Scan: Patch Planning" and "Roof Scan: Fitting Primary Surfaces to a Mesh" lessons, but only their titles were reachable ([Autodesk](https://www.autodesk.com/campaigns/alias-automotive-tutorials)). The corners where three transitions meet a primary are built last as ball corners or corner blends. These are tangent or curvature continuous to the fillets and the primary, and they rebuild when the fillets change ([Alias Ball Corner](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2022/ENU/Alias-NURBS-Modeling/files/Create-geometry/Build-corner-tertiary-surfaces/GUID-E2E78F2A-62FD-4B98-AE8C-E0F126430A9D.html); [Alias 2025.1 Corner Blend](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2025/ENU/Alias-WhatsNew/files/wn-whatsnewinalias2025/wn-alias-corner-blend-2025-1.html)). Rhino adds that you should not build from trimmed edges ([Rhino](https://www.rhino3d.com/docs/guides/industrial-design/surface-modeling-tips/)). That is the rule behind "oversize, keep the untrimmed base, trim last".

Applied to the AE86 (our inference), this gives **one** roof face. It is a height field over the roof's best-fit plane, with its parameter rectangle extended **20-40 mm** past the drip lines and both header seams. No source states an overshoot. The face should be full width and symmetric about the centreline by construction: give it mirror-paired poles, or fit it to the vertices of both halves. Do not fit a half and mirror it. Rhino's guide says a mirrored half is only G1 by its control-point rule. Our own reading is that symmetry does force equal curvature, but it flips the curvature's rate of change, leaving a ridge in the curvature map along the centreline. A symmetric full-width surface has neither problem. If the pipeline still wants half-car faces, split that one surface at y = 0, and both halves stay the same exact surface. A degree 5 single span (6 × 6 poles) is the Class-A-style candidate. The pipeline's cross-validated cubic is the RE-style one. Run both through the same blocked cross-validation and keep the simpler model if it is within 5 %, which is the pipeline's existing rule.

## Every neighbour takes its edge and slope from the roof

The published tolerance numbers are few. Alias gives a typical maximum **G0 gap of 0.01 to 0.001 mm** for production. It says a **tangent break below 0.1°** "will normally not be visible", and it defines G2 deviation as (R1 − R2)/(R1 + R2) without a pass value ([Alias construction tolerances](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2016/ENU/Alias-Tutorials/files/GUID-2FCE06EB-8EF7-4507-92F7-82A73A0DF378.htm)). A proposed Class B standard quotes G0 0.001 mm and G1 0.2° ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_A_surface)). Class-A exteriors target G2 at visible seams and G3 along long highlights ([Novedge](https://novedge.com/blogs/design-news/design-software-history-surface-interrogation-in-cad-history-techniques-and-industrialized-fairness-analysis)). No source gives continuity rules for drip rails or header radii, so the per-join targets in the table are our reasoning from the primary/secondary/tertiary hierarchy, not sourced standards.

| Join on the AE86 | Role | How it is built in our pipeline | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roof ↔ drip-rail strip (r7, r21) | Secondary feature | Re-run `strip_build.py` after the roof, so the strip's roof-side rail *is* the roof's built edge (0.01-0.02 mm, as on the rear quarter) | G0 exact (shared edge); a crease is honest where Kunos has one; the roof should already roll off, so the strip is not doing the turning |
| Roof ↔ windscreen header | Secondary blend | Rolled-edge band built *from* the roof (skin minus a drop profile), trimmed on the same curve | G2 on the roof side: G0 ≤ 0.01 mm, G1 ≤ 0.1°, Δκ ≤ 5 % |
| Roof ↔ hatch header | Secondary blend | As the windscreen header, then the hatch shut line | As above |
| Roof ↔ C-pillar top (r1x00) | Primary to primary | Roof is master: refit the r1x00 junction face with roof samples beside the seam as data, or add G1/G2 rows on the roof along the pillar's top line | G2; the long side highlight runs across it, and the faint 0.36 / 1.15 mm line there must go |
| A-pillar top, roof + drip + header corners | Tertiary corners | Small setback patches, `MakeFilling` G1, at most 4 clean sides | G1; G0 acceptable where the corner is smaller than about the strip width |

Two consequences matter for the build order. First, the C-pillar was built before the roof, so the master/slave relationship has to be inverted at its top. The pipeline already has the tool for this: on the rear quarter, the minor panel was "fitted after the main one with samples of the main's finished surfaces as data beside the seam" ([PANEL_FIT_TECHNIQUE.md](file:///D:/Claude/Claude-/blender/kn5-study/PANEL_FIT_TECHNIQUE.md)). Second, for G2 at the headers, the drop profile of a rolled-edge band must start with **zero value, slope and curvature**, for example a profile growing as t³ from the trim line. The pipeline's present rule fixes only value and slope, which gives G1. This is our inference from the definition of G2, and it has not been tested.

## 150 mm chords sit a millimetre inside the roof

A facet edge of length L on radius R sags by about **s ≈ L²/(8R)**. That is **0.94 mm for 150 mm on R = 3 m and 0.56 mm on R = 5 m**, and facet interiors are worse because the triangle spans a diagonal. We ran a synthetic test sized like the roof: a 1400 × 1100 mm crowned quadric, an 80-vertex / 126-triangle mesh with ~150 mm edges, and vertices exactly on the truth. In it, **flat facet interiors sat 1.3 mm low on average and 2.56 mm at worst** ([sag_test.py, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/sag_test.py)). Feeding those samples to an 8 × 6 cubic fit at equal weight biased it by **−1.26 mm**. Weighting the vertices ×20 still left **−0.67 mm**. Vertices alone gave **0.005 mm RMS** ([sag_test.py, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/sag_test.py)). Down-weighting scales the bias but never removes it. The commercial tools never meet this problem because they assume dense scans where any facet point is on the surface. None of the documentation we found has a mode for coarse chordal meshes. Pottmann and Leopoldseder's analysis gives the theory: plain point distance is a far-field error. Near the surface, the right error is the distance to the tangent plane, and parameter estimation "largely effects the result" ([Pottmann & Leopoldseder](https://www.dmg.tuwien.ac.at/geom/ig/papers/pot129.pdf)).

This overturns one shortcut that is still pending in the pipeline. The blanket "corner normals within ~4°" flat-facet test would call most roof facets flat and feed in exactly the biased samples above. The sag-based threshold **θ_max = 8τ/L** gives **0.9° for τ = 0.3 mm on a 150 mm facet**, while a 150 mm edge on R = 3-5 m turns 1.7-2.9°. So, as we derive it, nearly every roof facet is a chord and contributes no interior samples. The roof is the opposite of the C-pillar top, which had no vertex across it and needed its flat facets as data ([Smooth region segmentation into faces](Smooth%20region%20segmentation%20into%20faces.md)).

Where extra interior data is wanted, the curved-patch interpolants work, but only as well as the normals they use. **PN triangles** reproduce vertex positions and normals ([Vlachos et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/364338.364387); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point-normal_triangle)). **Phong tessellation** projects onto the vertex tangent planes and blends with a shape factor α ([Boubekeur & Alexa](https://www.telecom-paristech.fr/~boubek/papers/PhongTessellation/)). In our test, PN with exact normals came within 0.006 mm. Phong was exact at **α = ½** (max 0.007 mm), but overshot by half the sag at the visual default α = ¾ (+0.66 mm mean) and by the full sag at α = 1. Our derivation explains why: on a quadric, projecting onto both end tangent planes moves the chord midpoint by twice the sag. With **mesh-averaged normals**, both methods fell to **0.28 mm RMS, 1 mm max, −0.17 mm mean bias** ([sag_test2.py, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/sag_test2.py)). They are therefore secondary data at σ ≈ 0.3 mm, and they never go on boundary rows. The steadiest single extra sample is the Hermite (PN) edge midpoint, which uses only the two normals on its own edge.

Normal rows follow the same logic. The averaged normals of a perfect quadric mesh were **0.18° off on average (0.48° max) inside and 1.2° (1.7° max) on the boundary**. Adding slope rows at weight 100 mm² *raised* the RMS from 0.005 to 0.033 mm, and at 10⁴ to 0.45 mm. With exact normals and 0.05 mm vertex noise, a heavy weight did help ([sag_test2.py, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/sag_test2.py)). Our rule is inverse-variance weighting, **w_n ≈ (σ_z/σ_θ)²**, which is about 90 mm² for σ_z 0.05 mm and σ_θ 0.3°. It applies only on interior vertices whose face ring is consistent, and only after measuring σ_θ: fit vertices only, then compare each stored Kunos normal with the fitted normal. A 1° tilt over 150 mm is 2.6 mm of height, so trusting game normals blindly costs more than it gains.

Fairing is the second silent bias. The standard simplified thin-plate energy ∫(x_uu² + 2x_uv² + x_vv²) ([Pottmann & Leopoldseder](https://www.dmg.tuwien.ac.at/geom/ig/papers/pot129.pdf); patent use in [US 10614178](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10614178)) penalises curvature itself. A crowned roof is nothing but curvature. With 0.1 mm vertex noise, thin-plate at λ = 10², 10³ and 10⁴ shifted the crown by **−0.05, −0.20 and −0.70 mm**. A **third-derivative energy** (S_uuu², S_uuv², S_uvv², S_vvv²) stayed at about −0.02 mm bias across λ = 10⁵-10¹⁰, and it cut the RMS from 0.075 to 0.033 mm ([sag_test3.py, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/sag_test3.py)). Control-net second differences at a "light" weight cost 0.3-0.5 mm even on noise-free data. Third differences on clamped knots did worse, because the end spans are non-uniform ([sag_test.py, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/sag_test.py)). A separate FreeCAD-side least-squares fit confirmed the scale: λ = 1e-3 flattened its crown by 21 mm ([t2_lsq.txt, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/t2_lsq.txt)). The literature leaves λ selection open. GCV is the common default but can have local minima ([arXiv 2205.15157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.15157)), and there is "little agreement" on methods for real data ([MDPI Stats](https://mdpi.com/2571-905X/4/3/42)). Pottmann reduces λ from iteration to iteration ([Pottmann & Leopoldseder](https://www.dmg.tuwien.ac.at/geom/ig/papers/pot129.pdf)). Our recommendation is to scan λ over decades with blocked cross-validation, and to choose the largest λ whose vertex RMS stays near the known vertex noise of 0.02-0.05 mm (the discrepancy principle). The pipeline measured that vertex noise directly: the lower door skin fits its vertices to 0.08 mm ([PANEL_FIT_TECHNIQUE.md](file:///D:/Claude/Claude-/blender/kn5-study/PANEL_FIT_TECHNIQUE.md)).

No public, numeric scan-to-Class-A tolerance exists. The modeller "will be given a tolerance" per programme, and the job is to keep the character and filter the flaws ([Autodesk Alias 2024](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2024/ENU/Alias-Getting-Started/files/theory-builders/GUID-64611955-D2CC-44F2-98F0-D4F1FE931D8B.html)). The often quoted 0.1-0.5 mm band could not be traced to a primary source. For this mesh, the data itself sets the limit. Vertices are exact, facet interiors are uncertain by up to the sag, and normals are good to a few tenths of a degree. One consequence for our scoring is easy to miss (our inference). The whole-build "area within 1 mm" metric measures distance to Kunos facets, so a *correct* roof will sit 0.6-2.6 mm above its chord facets. For the roof, coverage within 3 mm should be essentially 100 %, but coverage within 1 mm is not a valid target. Judge facet distance against the predicted sag instead.

## FreeCAD 1.1.4: own least squares fits, `Part.Face` trims, `sewShape` closes

Measured on Nik's PC with an analytic roof: 1400 mm long, 65 mm crown, 29 mm camber, tumblehome, an asymmetry and a 3 mm bump, plus four G2-exact neighbour strips. `Part.BSplineSurface.approximate` (GeomAPI_PointsToBSplineSurface) only accepts a rectangular grid ([OCCT](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_geom_a_p_i___points_to_b_spline_surface.html)). It fits a station × profile grid to **0.005 mm in 0.02 s**, needs **DegMax ≥ 5 for C2** (DegMax 3 with C2 fails with "Surface not done"), and gives 482 mm of garbage on reshaped scatter ([t1_fit.txt, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/t1_fit.txt)). For scattered vertices there are two working routes. The first is our own numpy least squares on fixed knots, handed to `buildFromPolesMultsKnots`. It reached **0.016 mm max on 2000 clean points in 0.4 s**, and **0.03 mm with G0 + G1 boundary rows** (weight 300·√(N/1000)). Its basis matched OCC's to 1e-14 ([t2_lsq.txt, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/t2_lsq.txt)). More spans hurt: 20 × 16 was worse than 12 × 10, and extra freedom wiggles at the edges unless the weights rise with it ([t4_g2_trim_sew.txt, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/t4_g2_trim_sew.txt)). True G2 comes from linearised curvature rows n·S_ij = II_neighbour, using the previous iterate's tangents, in 2 extra solves. At weight 30 the cross-edge curvature jump fell from **6 % to 0.4 % of κ**, with G1 0.0037° and dev 0.015 mm, in 3.3 s ([t4_g2_trim_sew.txt, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/t4_g2_trim_sew.txt)). This is the pipeline's own `panel_fit.py` approach, extended, and it is the right primary route.

The second route is GeomPlate. `BRepOffsetAPI.MakeFilling` with neighbour edges at order 1 plus free points reached **0.002 mm and G1 0.0045°** on 300 points. But it is degree 8 with 23 × 23 poles, its run time grows roughly with the cube of the constraint count (0.16 s, 1.4 s and 7 s for 300, 1000 and 2000 points), and it needs a closed loop of existing neighbour edges ([t3_plate_0_300.txt, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/t3_plate_0_300.txt); [t3_plate_2000.txt, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/t3_plate_2000.txt)). The roof's headers are not built yet, so that loop does not exist. Three binding traps are confirmed from FreeCAD's source. First, `GeomPlate.CurveConstraint` takes only a bare curve, so Order 1 raises "Curve must be on a Surface". Second, MakeFilling's order 2 is `static_cast` to the enum value for C1, which the plate ignores, so it behaves exactly like order 0, and real G2 is refused ([FreeCAD MakeFilling binding](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/blob/main/src/Mod/Part/App/BRepOffsetAPI_MakeFillingPyImp.cpp); [CurveConstraint binding](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/blob/main/src/Mod/Part/App/GeomPlate/CurveConstraintPyImp.cpp); [OCCT BRepFill_Filling](https://github.com/Open-Cascade-SAS/OCCT/blob/V7_8_0/src/BRepFill/BRepFill_Filling.cxx)). Third, `Part.PlateSurface` ignores its `Curves` argument and crashed freecadcmd on 424 points ([PlateSurfacePyImp.cpp](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/blob/main/src/Mod/Part/App/PlateSurfacePyImp.cpp); [t5_sew_split_pt.txt, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/t5_sew_split_pt.txt)). The plate's own `G1Error()` read 0.0000 where our sampler measured 0.35°. This supersedes the `G1Error()` pass test proposed in the C-pillar report: never use it as the check. MakeFilling stays useful for the small corner patches and as an independent cross-check of the main fit.

Trimming had one clear winner in the local test ([t4_g2_trim_sew.txt, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/t4_g2_trim_sew.txt); [t6_pcurve_sew.txt, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/t6_pcurve_sew.txt)):

| Trim method | Valid | Edges | Boundary dev | Sewed? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `Part.Face(srf, Part.Wire(Part.__sortEdges__(edges3d)))` + `validate()` | yes | 4 | 0.0000 mm | **yes** (only one) |
| Own pcurves (`srf.parameter` samples → `BSplineCurve2d.interpolate` → `toShape(srf)`) | yes | 4 | 0.0002 mm | no, 20 free edges, even after `fix` |
| `generalFuse(edges3d, 1e-3)` | yes | 6 | 0.0000 mm | corner edges split |
| `BOPTools slice` with extruded walls | yes | 7 | 0.0002 mm | corner edges split |
| `Face.project` + `makeSplitShape` | yes | 4 | **113 mm off**, wrong piece | — |

This matters because the pipeline's current trim (2D curves → `toShape` → `Part.Face`) is the pcurve route that refused to sew. The root cause was not found, so the 3D-edge form should be the roof's default until a sew test clears the pcurve form. Sewing the roof and four neighbours with `sewShape(1e-6 … 1e-2)` produced one shell with only the outer border free. `MeshPart.meshFromShape` of it gave a single manifold component, against three pieces for the unsewn compound ([t5_sew_split_pt.txt, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/t5_sew_split_pt.txt)). Continuity is checked with our own sampler, since FreeCAD does not bind `LocalAnalysis` ([OCCT 7.8](https://dev.opencascade.org/doc/occt-7.8.0/refman/html/classLocalAnalysis__SurfaceContinuity.html)). It samples 41 points per shared edge, takes (u, v) on each face from `Surface.parameter`, and compares positions, normals, and normal curvatures from `getDN` in the cross-edge direction. It cleanly separated a G2-by-construction fit (Δκ 3.3e-6 against κ 3.3e-4), a G1 plate (15 % of κ) and a C0 plate (0.35°) ([common.py, local](file:///D:/ClaudeData/tmp/research_roof/occ/common.py)). For a fairness sign-off, Novedge recommends zebra, isophotes, environment maps and mean/Gaussian curvature maps, with dense *exact* sampling, because coarse tessellation fakes or hides waviness ([Novedge](https://novedge.com/blogs/design-news/design-software-history-surface-interrogation-in-cad-history-techniques-and-industrialized-fairness-analysis)). Rhino adds: measure with EdgeContinuity, do not trust zebra alone, and insert a knot at the worst point rather than refining globally ([Rhino](https://www.rhino3d.com/docs/guides/industrial-design/surface-modeling-tips/)).

```python
# trim on 3D edges that lie on the roof surface, sew, check (pattern measured in t4/t5)
w = Part.Wire(Part.__sortEdges__(trim_edges))      # drip rails, header lines, C-pillar top
roof = Part.Face(srf, w); roof.validate(); assert roof.isValid()
body = Part.Compound([roof, drip_L, drip_R, hdr_front, hdr_rear, pillar_top]); body.sewShape(0.01)
free = [e for e in body.Edges if len(body.ancestorsOfType(e, Part.Face)) < 2]   # must be the outer border only
mesh = MeshPart.meshFromShape(Shape=body, MaxLength=6)                          # even mesh for zebra
```

## A sixteen-step build for the AE86 roof

The recipe below is our synthesis for the existing pipeline (`panel_fit.py` → `panel_fit_freecad.py` → `strip_build.py` → `regress.sh`). Sourced numbers are marked in the sections above. Everything else is a starting value to tune on the real roof.

| # | Step | Parameters (starting values) | Acceptance check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Clip the roof region from `GEO_body`; list neighbours: drip strips r7/r21 each side, C-pillar r1x00, windscreen and hatch header bands, A-pillar tops | Crease > 20° splits regions as today | Roof is one region per side of the creases, no fragments |
| 2 | Mirror the vertices to full width (or take both halves) | Symmetry plane y = 0 | Mirrored vertex pairs within 0.1 mm |
| 3 | Classify every facet edge with `edge_sag` (L²/8R, a_i/a_j signs) and each facet by θ_max = 8τ/L | τ = 0.3 mm → ≈0.9° at 150 mm | Expect almost all roof facets "chord"; no interior facet samples from them |
| 4 | Measure stored normals: vertices-only trial fit, then the angle between each Kunos normal and the fit normal | Interior vertices only | σ_θ known; use normal rows only if σ_θ ≤ 0.2-0.3° |
| 5 | Frame and domain: height field over the best-fit plane; parameter rectangle = outline + overshoot | Overshoot 20-40 mm; mirror-paired poles | Unsupported corners of the rectangle stay within 2 mm of the extended crown (watch for the 2-7 mm wiggles seen in `t2_lsq`) |
| 6 | Data rows: all roof vertices; built C-pillar surface samples within 30 mm beyond the seam; next Kunos vertex row beyond the drip and header lines (≤ 50 mm out, low weight); optional PN edge midpoints | Vertices σ 0.05 mm; neighbour samples 0.02 mm; PN midpoints σ 0.3 mm, interior edges only; normal rows w_n = (σ_z/σ_θ)² | No chord-facet samples anywhere |
| 7 | Fairing: integrated third-derivative energy (quadrature or Gram matrix), not control-net differences | λ scanned over decades | Largest λ with vertex RMS ≤ 0.05 mm |
| 8 | Model choice by blocked 5-fold CV: degree 5 single span (6 × 6) vs cubic 4-8 controls per direction | Blocks ≈ one facet (150-200 mm), not 40 mm | Simplest within 5 % of the best held-out score |
| 9 | Bias and roll-off check | Interior vertices; centreline and edge rows | Signed mean residual \|≤ 0.05 mm\|; vertex RMS ≤ 0.1 mm, max ≤ 0.3 mm; held-out RMS ≤ 0.2-0.3 mm; edge-row residuals show no systematic sign (crown acceleration kept) |
| 10 | C-pillar join, roof as master: refit r1x00 with roof samples beside the seam, or add G1/G2 rows on the roof along the pillar top line | G1 rows 300·√(N/1000); G2 weight 30, 2 linearised iterations | G0 ≤ 0.01 mm, G1 ≤ 0.1° (aim 0.05°), Δκ ≤ 5 % of κ |
| 11 | Trim curves: drip line each side = the strip's chain side curve; header lines from the Kunos boundary resampled every 5 mm; all as 3D edges lying on the roof (project samples with `srf.parameter`, refit) | Fewest poles within 0.4 mm, as today | Each curve within 0.001 mm of the roof surface |

## Conclusion

The roof changes what "fit" means in this pipeline. On the door and quarter, the facets were close enough to the surface that coverage within 1 mm was a fair score. On the roof, the facets are the *wrong* answer by about a millimetre, and both standard instincts push the result toward them: adding facet samples, and adding thin-plate smoothing. The correct roof therefore scores *worse* on the facet metric than a biased one. The decisive checks become vertex residual, signed crown bias, and residual against predicted sag. The other shift is topological. The C-pillar, drip strips and headers were built or planned as fits in their own right, but the professional order makes them derivatives of the roof. The pipeline already has the tools for this (strips on built edges, minor panels fitted with the master's samples). It only needs to apply them in the opposite direction at the pillar top.

The open risks are concrete. The decisive experiments are synthetic: a quadric and an analytic roof with exact vertices. A real roof with varying curvature favours third-derivative fairing less, and Kunos may have authored, smoothing-group-averaged or hand-edited normals, which steps 3-4 must measure before any normal or PN data is used. Third-derivative fairing resists curvature *change*, so at a large λ it can work against the crown acceleration toward the drip line that Class-A wants, and step 9's edge-row check guards against that. The failure of the pcurve-trimmed face to sew was not root-caused, and the pipeline's current trim uses that route, so a five-minute `sewShape` / `isSame` test on the roof is the first thing to run. G2 is reachable only through our own least-squares rows, because FreeCAD's MakeFilling cannot do G2, and the corner patches have so far been tested only on a 4-sided hole. A 5-sided A-pillar corner may need splitting. Finally, the paywalled Autodesk "Roof Scan" and LearningAlias lessons probably hold the real patch layouts and degrees. The single-span-degree-5 versus cubic choice is left to cross-validation rather than settled by a source.
