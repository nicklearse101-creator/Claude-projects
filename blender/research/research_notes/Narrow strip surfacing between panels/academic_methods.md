# Academic methods: narrow strip detection and shared-boundary surfacing in mesh-to-B-rep reverse engineering

Scope note: about 16 searches and fetches. Several key PDFs (Kós et al. blend radius at Cardiff ORCA, Lai et al. TVCG 2007) could not be read: one returned 403 and one came back as binary. Where only abstracts or index records were available, this is said explicitly. The richest primary source with concrete detail turned out to be the Geomagic patent US8004517B1 (Edelsbrunner, Facello, Gloth, Terék, Várady). It is the industrial form of the Várady-group "feature skeleton / connecting feature / vertex blend" pipeline, and it was read in full up to character 200k.

## Q1. Classic RE pipeline papers: how they detect and rebuild fillets, chamfers and steps

### Takeaway
The classic Várady/Martin/Benkő/Kós school treats a CAD-like object as a hierarchy: **primary surfaces** (large, simple or free-form), **connecting features** (fillets, blends, chamfers: long four-sided strips between two primaries), and **vertex blends** (n-sided patches where strips meet). The pipeline segments first and finds the strip structure as a curve network (a "feature skeleton"), which is then "thickened" into two boundary curves per strip. Primaries are fitted first, under constraints. The strips are rebuilt last, either as rolling-ball blends defined by the primaries plus a radius, or as fitted strips.

### Cited Findings
- Várady, Martin & Cox, "Reverse engineering of geometric models – an introduction", *Computer-Aided Design* 29(4):255–268, 1997, DOI 10.1016/S0010-4485(96)00054-1. It is the standard survey of the RE process (segmentation, surface fitting for simple and free-form shapes, multi-view merging, B-rep construction), and it notes that solutions were still limited at the time. About 993 Scopus citations are listed. — [ORCA Cardiff record](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/48124/); [dblp](https://dblp.dagstuhl.de/rec/journals/cad/VaradyMC97.html)
- Benkő, Martin & Várady, "Algorithms for reverse engineering boundary representation models", *CAD* 33(11):839–851, 2001. It presents four key components: (1) efficient segmentation of point data into regions; (2) translational and rotational surfaces with smooth, constrained profiles; (3) building B-rep topology; (4) **adding blends last**. — [SZTAKI eprint](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/2654/); [author PDF (not read)](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/1811/1/REalgorithms.pdf)
- A related Cardiff paper by Langbein et al. detects fixed-radius rolling-ball blends in the point data but **does not insert them immediately**. Blends are stored as edge and vertex attributes, and the blend surfaces are built after "beautification" (constraint-based regularisation) of the primaries. — [Langbein et al., SM 2004 (via search snippet)](https://diglib7.eg.org/bitstream/handle/10.2312/sm20041398/255-260_langbein.pdf)
- Benkő & Várady, "Segmentation methods for smooth point regions of conventional engineering objects", *CAD* 36(6):511–523, 2004. Segmentation is "relatively simple if regions are bounded by sharp edges and small blends". The hard case is separating smoothly connected regions. Their direct segmentation is a **hierarchy of simple tests in three phases** using local geometric and statistical indicators ("statistical similarity tests"), and it also detects linear extrusion and revolution surfaces. The resulting multi-region structure feeds constrained fitting. The thresholds themselves were not visible in the abstract. — [Bohrium abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/segmentation-methods-for-smooth-point-regions-of-conventional-engineering-objects/812389137507155969-2539); [dblp](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/cad/BenkV04.html)
- Kós, Martin & Várady, "Methods to recover constant radius rolling ball blends in reverse engineering", *CAGD* 17(2):127–160, 2000, DOI 10.1016/S0167-8396(99)00043-6. It compares methods for estimating the blend radius. Once the radius is known **and the primary surfaces are known**, the whole blend surface can be computed. Variable-radius and vertex blends were left to later work. — [SZTAKI eprint](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/2459/); [dblp](https://dblp.org/rec/journals/cagd/KosMV99)
- Follow-up: Kós, "Recovering variable radius rolling ball blends in reverse engineering" (title only seen). — [SZTAKI eprint](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/2648)
- Andor, Benkő, Kós, Martin & Várady, "Constrained fitting in reverse engineering", *CAGD* 19(3):173–205, 2002, DOI 10.1016/S0167-8396(01)00085-1. It fits several curves and surfaces **simultaneously with constraints between their parameters** (tangency, parallelism, symmetry and so on). Auto-generated constraints conflict, so the paper gives a numerical method to resolve the conflicts, plus compact representations and approximate distance functions so each iteration is cheap. — [Zhenzhi abstract](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/900966726); [SZTAKI](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/2967/); [author PDF (not read)](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/1812/1/ConstrainedFitting.pdf)
- Várady, Facello & Terék, "Automatic extraction of surface structures in digital shape reconstruction", *CAD* 39(5):379–388, 2007, DOI 10.1016/j.cad.2007.02.011. It builds a **feature skeleton**, a curve network on the mesh that encodes the region structure (primaries, connecting features, vertex blends). The skeleton is then thickened, with boundary placement driven by local curvature. — [search summary citing the paper and the patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)
- Terék & Várady, "Setback vertex blends in digital shape reconstruction", *Mathematics of Surfaces XIII*, 2009. It reconstructs parts as primary surfaces + connecting features (fillets) + vertex blends. Setback vertex blends resolve self-intersecting segmenting curve networks. — [Unpaywall/Springer record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2F978-3-642-03596-8_21)
- Later I-patch work (Sipos, Várady, Salvi, Vaitkus 2020, *Computers & Graphics*) builds setback vertex blends with conic boundaries on the Várady–Benkő–Kós–Rockwood I-patch formulation. — [Sipos I-patch PDF](https://3dgeo.iit.bme.hu/~sipos/writings/ipatch.pdf); [Sipos dissertation](https://3dgeo.iit.bme.hu/~sipos/writings/dissertation.pdf)
- Geomagic patent US8004517B1 (Edelsbrunner, Facello, Gloth, Terék, Várady; priority 24 Jun 2005, granted 23 Aug 2011). Full 4-stage pipeline:
  1. **Morse segmentation.** Piecewise-linear indicator functions on the vertices (curvature, slippability via a 6×6 matrix, sweepability, proximity, a "translational" indicator built from the ratio of principal curvatures), used alone or as a weighted sum. A watershed builds the regions, O(n α(n)). Saddle–extremum pairs are cancelled in order of persistence, and a sensitivity threshold picks the level.
  2. **Separator sets** (the strip regions) are triangle strips grown from the Morse boundary polylines "until the translational strength falls below a certain threshold" (no number given).
  3. **Feature skeleton.** There are three edge classes: *feature edges* through highly curved transitions (temporary, later thickened), *sharp edges* (intersections of primaries, extracted precisely; normals in a neighbourhood tested for splitting into two clusters at fine-to-coarse scales, then "edge sharpening" with a narrow two-sided grid fit), and *subdividing edges* (cut large smooth regions, not thickened). Tracing follows a local translational vector field estimated from **about 20–30 points per vertex**, integrated like an ODE (Runge–Kutta). *Guided tracing* is used where the field vanishes (near vertices), keeping the end tangents. *Balanced tracing* is an affine mix of the two.
  4. **Thickening.** Each smooth feature edge becomes **two longitudinal boundary curves (trimlines)**, and each vertex becomes a loop of profile curves + spring curves.
  
  — [Google Patents US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)
- Surface representations listed in the patent: primaries are a quadrangular tiling or a trimmed surface. Connecting features are a "double strip of quadrangles" or special surfaces (single- or variable-radius rolling-ball blends). Vertex blends are a composite of quadrangles sharing a centre point, or a single trimmed surface constrained to its neighbours. — [US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)

### Inferences
- Across this school the common thread is to **fit the primaries first and derive the strip from them**. A strip is either (a) a rolling-ball blend computed exactly from the two primaries plus a radius (so its boundary curves are the ball contact curves, exactly on both primaries), or (b) a four-sided fitted strip bounded by the two trimlines and two profile/spring curves.
- Chamfers and "steps" are not treated separately in the sources I could read. In this framework a chamfer is a connecting feature whose cross-section is a line, and a step/flange is a primary region of small width. Both go through the same skeleton → thickening → four-sided strip route.

### Gaps
- Exact segmentation thresholds in Benkő & Várady 2004 and the radius-estimation method comparison in Kós et al. 2000 (PDF blocked with 403): not retrieved.
- The full text of Várady, Facello & Terék 2007 was not read. Its details are inferred from the patent with the same authors.
- Werghi et al. (constraint-based RE of planar/quadric objects with blends) was not searched because of the call budget.

## Q2. Detecting a narrow strip automatically: width, aspect ratio, curvature/normal tests, robustness on sparse meshes

### Takeaway
The proven approach is **cross-sectional**: grow a separator strip around a high-curvature or "translational" ridge, then find its two edges as the **two cross-sectional minima** of a similarity indicator on either side of the midline (the midline is the cross-sectional maximum). Width falls out of the boundary placement. Multi-scale normal-clustering and integral-invariant tests separate sharp edges (width → 0) from blends. Self-intersections of the thickened strips are fixed with (weighted) Voronoi relocation, or by collapsing the strip into a vertex blend.

### Cited Findings
- **Boundary placement (patent).** Feature boundaries sit at the "two adjacent cross-sectional minima" of a similarity indicator, and the feature midline at the cross-sectional maximum. Tracing starts in the middle of the feature (the most stable point) and goes left and right, combining the local translational direction, the midline's guiding direction and a stabilisation factor that stops oscillation. — [US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)
- **Width = distance between the boundaries.** Setback distances at vertices come from the *maximum width of the neighbouring features* (for example, setback sb1 uses the widths of edges e2 and e3), plus an optional positive correction for aesthetics or degenerate cases. No numeric defaults are given. — [US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)
- **Rolling-ball alternative.** If a connecting feature fits a rolling-ball model, its boundaries are defined by a spine curve plus a constant or varying radius. — [US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)
- **Sigmoid curvature normalisation.** Indicator values are shifted by a curvature bound through tansig/logsig functions. A vertex-blend indicator uses a principal-curvature-ratio threshold, but its value is not stated. — [US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)
- **Narrow strips that degenerate.** Where the transition is narrow enough, a longitudinal boundary may vanish. The vertex blends on both sides then merge, and an artificial spring curve joins the remaining boundaries. Zero-length curve elements are allowed. Self-intersections of the thickened loops: *Strategy 1* keeps the topology and moves the thickened edges with a weighted Voronoi diagram so they do not enter opposite regions, then smooths. *Strategy 2* merges the interfering parts into a complex middle vertex blend with new spring curves. It separates better but loses the one-to-one topology. — [US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)
- **Alternative structures for strips meeting at vertices.** *Model A* uses primaries + connecting features + setback vertex blends (at most 2n sides for n edges). *Model B* uses quadrangular connecting features with no spring curves, so each vertex blend is exactly n-sided. *Model C* has no vertex blends, and each strip quadrangle carries two end pieces. — [US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)
- **Lai, Zhou, Hu, Wallner & Pottmann**, "Robust feature classification and editing", *IEEE TVCG* 13(1):34–45, 2007. Feature-sensitive isotropic remeshing comes first, then features are detected **at multiple scales with integral invariants of local neighbourhoods**. Morphological and smoothing operations then extract feature regions and classify them as ridges, valleys and prongs. A sharp edge is framed as the limit of a blended edge as the blend radius tends to 0. — [TU Wien page](https://www.geometrie.tuwien.ac.at/ig/publications/oldpub/2007/lzhwp_robust_07/lzhwp_robust_07.html)
- **Lai, Zhou, Hu & Martin**, "Feature sensitive mesh segmentation", SPM 2006. Hierarchical top-down clustering on a feature-sensitive remeshed hierarchy. It uses **only normal information, not dihedral angles**, because dihedral-angle computation is noise-sensitive. The aim is regions that each fit one analytic surface. — [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/5213); [PDF](https://mysite.cs.cf.ac.uk/Yukun.Lai/papers/fsseg.pdf)
- Lai et al.'s random-walk segmentation names "each piece bounded by sharp edges or small radius blends" as a target criterion for CAD meshes. — [ORCA PDF (snippet)](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/5281/1/RandomWalkSegJ.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a sparse low-poly game mesh with long thin triangles (the AC/kn5 case), dihedral-angle and per-vertex curvature estimates are unreliable. The literature's answers are (a) remesh isotropically / feature-sensitively first (Lai et al.), (b) use normal-only clustering, or integral invariants over a ball of radius r, which do not need fine triangles, and (c) estimate direction from roughly 20–30 neighbouring points, not 1-ring neighbours (patent).
- A practical narrow-strip test consistent with these sources: build the two candidate boundary polylines, then take width w(s) = distance between the boundaries along the cross-section at arc length s, and strip length L. Flag the region as a connecting strip when max w ≪ L (aspect ratio) and the normal turns between the two boundaries in one sense (fillet/chamfer) or turns twice (step). The aspect-ratio threshold is my suggestion. **No paper I read gave a numeric aspect-ratio threshold.**
- Using the Voronoi/medial axis to keep thickened boundaries from crossing (patent Strategy 1) amounts to using the medial axis of the strip as its midline. This is consistent with a "midline = cross-section maximum" definition.

### Gaps
- No source found with numeric width or aspect-ratio thresholds, or with geodesic-distance-based width estimation for strips, specifically tested on sparse meshes.
- The integral-invariant radii and thresholds in Lai et al. 2007 were not retrieved (the PDF came back as binary).

## Q3. Fitting one fair 3D B-spline curve to the shared boundary, then trimming both neighbours on it

### Takeaway
The literature offers two routes. (1) **Derive the shared curve from the surfaces**: intersect the two fitted primaries (sharp edges, as in Point2CAD), or compute the rolling-ball contact curves from the primaries plus the radius (blends). The edge then lies exactly on both surfaces by construction. (2) **Fit the curve first** to the traced boundary polyline, by least squares plus fairing with foot-point iteration, ideally SDM. Then constrain or trim the neighbouring surfaces to it, using simultaneous constrained fitting (Andor/Benkő et al. 2002), or B-spline fitting where the feature curve must be a parameter line (Lai, Hu, Pottmann).

### Cited Findings
- **Wang, Pottmann & Liu**, "Fitting B-spline curves to point clouds by squared distance minimization (SDM)", *ACM TOG* 25(2):214–238, 2006. It compares three error terms. Point distance (PDM) is an alternating foot-point / least-squares method with linear convergence. Tangent distance (TDM) is a Gauss–Newton method that is **unstable where curvature varies a lot**. SDM uses a curvature-based quadratic approximant of squared distance and amounts to a quasi-Newton method with a positive-definite Hessian approximation. It converges faster and more stably. The paper shows planar curves. — [TU Wien page](https://www.geometrie.tuwien.ac.at/geom/ig/publications/oldpub/2006/wpl_curves_06/wpl_curves_06.html); [tech report PDF](https://www.geometrie.tuwien.ac.at/geom/ig/papers/tr143.pdf); [HKU PDF](https://i.cs.hku.hk/GraphicsGroup/publications/pdf/Fitting%20B-spline%20Curves%20to%20Point%20Clouds%20by%20Curvature-Based%20Squared%20Distance%20Minimization.pdf)
- **Lai, Hu & Pottmann**, "Surface fitting based on a feature sensitive parametrization" (*CAD* 2006). To represent a sharp feature in a B-spline surface it **must be a parameter line**, so the parametrisation is built to align with the features before fitting. — [Bohrium abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/surface-fitting-based-on-a-feature-sensitive-parametrization/811924506372210692-2539)
- **Constrained simultaneous fitting.** Andor et al. 2002 fit several curves and surfaces together with constraints between parameters and resolve conflicting constraints numerically. This is the classic way to make neighbouring surfaces agree, for example tangent along a blend or meeting at a common edge. — [Zhenzhi abstract](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/900966726)
- In the patent pipeline, shared boundaries come from the thickened feature skeleton. Connecting features are four-sided (two primaries + two vertex blends), profile curves are **tangential to the adjacent primary regions**, and spring curves join two longitudinal boundaries smoothly. Guided tracing extends trimlines until they meet tangentially. Edges are smoothed by fairing the mesh and by curve tracing. — [US8004517B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8004517B1/en)
- **Point2CAD** recovers edges by **intersecting adjacent fitted surfaces** and recovers corners by intersecting adjacent edges. The edge quality is therefore bounded by the surface fits. — [Split-and-Fit, arXiv 2406.05261 (discussing Point2CAD)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.05261)

### Inferences
- These steps are standard B-spline practice from my background knowledge (Piegl & Tiller-style), not taken from the sources read. For the fit-curve-first route, a robust recipe is as follows.
  1. Order the boundary polyline samples.
  2. Initial parameters by chord length or centripetal (sqrt-chord) parametrisation.
  3. Cubic open uniform or averaged knot vector with few control points (start with about 6–10 per panel edge and add knots only where the residual exceeds tolerance).
  4. Minimise Σ‖C(t_i) − p_i‖² + λ∫‖C''‖² (or ‖C'''‖²) dt.
  5. Re-project the parameters (foot points) and repeat (PDM). Switch to SDM if convergence is slow on curved parts.
- To get **exact** common edges after fitting neighbouring B-spline surfaces separately, the clean options are:
  - (a) make the curve an isoparametric boundary of both patches (shared control row: the four-sided strip model of the patent, and the Lai/Hu/Pottmann principle);
  - (b) trim both surfaces by the same 3D curve, accepting tolerance-level gaps (normal trimmed-NURBS practice);
  - (c) for strips, build the strip surface from the two boundary curves themselves (ruled or loft), so it shares them exactly. The neighbouring panels are then trimmed on, or fitted with their boundary row fixed to, those same curves.
- For sharp creases where the two primaries are well fitted, intersecting them (Point2CAD) gives a curve exactly on both surfaces. For narrow strips this is unstable when the two surfaces are nearly tangent: the intersection is ill-conditioned at small angles. That is a reason to fit the boundary curve from the data instead.

### Gaps
- No paper found that explicitly fits one 3D B-spline to a mesh crease **and** then trims two independently fitted B-spline panels on it with stated tolerances. The steps above are assembled from general practice.
- Knot-placement rules from the SDM paper (control-point insertion criteria) were not visible on the abstract page.

## Q4. Ruled surface between two boundary curves: parameter pairing, twist, deviation checks

### Takeaway
The choice of correspondence between the two rails *is* the design variable. Different reparametrisations of the same rails give different ruled surfaces. The established approach (Wang & Tang and successors) is to discretise both rails and search a graph for the bridging map that minimises a cost such as total twist or normal variation (shortest path / dynamic programming), optionally followed by local point moves that increase developability. Elber's dynamic-programming ruled fitting is the counterpart for fitting rulings to existing surface data.

### Cited Findings
- **Wang & Tang (2005)**, "Optimal boundary triangulations of an interpolating ruled surface" (*J. Comput. Inf. Sci. Eng.*). It defines a discrete *boundary bridge triangulation* between two polygonal directrices and finds the best mapping as a **shortest path** in a graph. It offers six objectives: minimal area, **minimal total twist**, maximal convexity, minimal bending, minimal mean-curvature variation, and minimal normal variation, each weightable. A ruling is twist-free if all surface normals along it are parallel. If all rulings are twist-free, the surface is developable. — [Bohrium abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/optimal-boundary-triangulations-of-an-interpolating-ruled-surface/812131287790780417-4387); [author PDF](https://mewangcl.github.io/pubs/JCISEOptmTrgl.pdf)
- **Chen, Tang & Joneja (2011)**, "Design of developable interpolating strips". It finds the mapping that maximises developability (measured by normal twist along the bridge edges), then moves points to reduce total twist. Its local method is faster, and its global method converges to a better solution. — [Bohrium abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/design-of-developable-interpolating-strips/813275659949309952-19239)
- Follow-ups give quasi-developable Bézier/B-spline interpolation (Chen & Tang 2013) and "as-developable-as-possible" B-spline interpolation (Bo et al. 2020). — [search summary; JCST DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11390-022-0680-5)
- **Elber**, ruled-surface approximation/fitting of free-form surfaces by dynamic programming. This is a different problem: fitting rulings to a given surface, not interpolating two given curves. — [Technion PDF](https://gershon.cs.technion.ac.il/papers/RuledSrfFitting.pdf); [Elber RuledApproxSurface](https://cggc.cs.technion.ac.il/files/gallery-pdfs/Elber-RuledApproxSurface.pdf)

### Inferences
- A practical pairing recipe for a strip between two fitted rail curves A(s) and B(t):
  1. Sample both rails densely (for example 100–200 points each).
  2. Run the Wang–Tang dynamic programme over (i, j) with cost per step = twist (angle between the surface normals at the two ends of a ruling) + λ·ruling length. Monotonicity of the path guarantees **rulings never cross**.
  3. Fit a smooth monotone reparametrisation t = φ(s) to the DP path (for example a low-degree monotone B-spline).
  4. Build S(s, v) = (1 − v)·A(s) + v·B(φ(s)), and make it a tensor-product NURBS by reparametrising B so both rails share one knot vector (knot merging / degree-matched rails).
- Deviation check: project the strip's mesh vertices onto S, report max and RMS distance plus the max angle between the mesh normals and the surface normals. Refit or switch to a loft with a middle section if the strip's cross-section is visibly curved (a fillet rather than a chamfer).
- A fillet is not ruled. For curved strips, a rolling-ball / circular cross-section (Kós et al.) or a cubic loft with tangent conditions from both neighbours (like the patent's profile curves "tangential to adjacent primaries") is the matching representation. A ruled surface fits chamfers, flat steps and flanges.
- Plain arc-length pairing is the naive default. It produces visible twist when the two rails differ in length locally (curved strips, tapering widths), which is exactly why the DP/shortest-path approaches exist.

### Gaps
- No stated numeric twist or deviation tolerances were found in the abstracts read.

## Q5. Learning-based B-rep reconstruction (ComplexGen, Point2CAD, NerVE, ParSeNet, BRepGen): what they do about thin faces

### Takeaway
The learning methods predict or fit patches, then recover edges by surface intersection (Point2CAD) or by predicting corners, curves and patches jointly with a topology optimisation (ComplexGen). None that I found deals specifically with thin or narrow faces. They are documented as weaker on B-spline patches, topology consistency and local sampling quality, which are exactly the conditions under which narrow strips appear.

### Cited Findings
- **ComplexGen** (Guo et al., SIGGRAPH 2022, arXiv 2205.14573) predicts the B-rep as a chain complex (corners, curves, patches and their adjacency) and then refines it. It is noted to be more sensitive to local point sampling quality, and its outputs are often geometry–topology inconsistent. Refitting and intersecting its surfaces with a separate pipeline has been suggested as a fix. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.14573); [Emergent Mind open problem](https://api.emergentmind.com/open-problems/rectify-complexgen-geometry-topology-inconsistency)
- **Point2CAD** (2023/24, arXiv 2312.04962) segments, fits analytic and free-form surfaces per segment, **intersects adjacent surfaces to get edges and adjacent edges to get corners**. Split-and-Fit reports B-spline fitting as "unstable and occasionally yields implausible results", so its own experiments mostly use elementary primitives. — [Point2CAD arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04962); [Split-and-Fit arXiv 2406.05261](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.05261)
- In Split-and-Fit's comparison, ComplexGen scores Chamfer 0.1191 (vertices) / 0.0442 (surfaces), while HPNet+Point2CAD and SEDNet+Point2CAD score better on several metrics but have higher topological error. — [Split-and-Fit](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.05261)

### Inferences
- For the thin strips in this project, a learning pipeline would most likely either merge the strip into a neighbour (under-segmentation) or produce a sliver patch whose intersection edges are unstable. Intersecting nearly tangent surfaces is ill-conditioned. The classic "skeleton → thicken → four-sided strip" approach is the better match.

### Gaps
- NerVE (neural volumetric edges), ParSeNet and BRepGen were not covered by the searches (budget). I found no source discussing thin or narrow faces in any learning-based method.
