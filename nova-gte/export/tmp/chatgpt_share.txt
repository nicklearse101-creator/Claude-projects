How to make Claude much better at 3D modelling your 1989 Nova GTE

I think you've identified a genuine weakness in a text-first workflow: Claude can help plan and write the code for a 3D model, but it needs other tools to generate visual references, create geometry, inspect the results and correct modelling mistakes.

The good news is that you don't need to replace Claude. You can turn it into the central controller of a much more capable 3D workflow.

For your 1989 right-hand-drive Vauxhall Nova GTE project for Assetto Corsa, I'd combine Claude Code, Blender, image-generation tools, 3D reconstruction tools and automated testing.

Claude plans and coordinates. Image-generation tools create visual references. Blender creates and edits the actual model. 3D reconstruction tools provide starting geometry. Automated checks and screenshots let Claude inspect and improve the results.

2. The biggest upgrade: make Claude work in a visual feedback loop

Giving Claude access to Blender is only half the solution.

The next step is to let it inspect what it has built, compare it against your references and correct the result.

Without that loop, Claude might write a script that runs successfully but creates a dashboard that's too wide, has the wrong proportions or puts the steering wheel in the wrong place.

The workflow

Step 1 — Gather reference images

Collect genuine photographs of the original Nova GTE interior.

Prioritise:

Straight-on dashboard photographs.

Direct views of the instrument cluster.

Steering wheel close-ups.

Driver's-side pedal photographs.

Centre console and gear lever photographs.

Step 2 — Have Claude analyse the references

Which dimensions are known.

Which proportions can be estimated.

Which features remain uncertain.

Which parts should be separate objects.

Step 3 — Ask Claude to build one component

Step 4 — Render the result

Ask Claude to create a repeatable render from the same camera angle as your reference.

Ask Claude to identify the largest visible discrepancies and make a small, targeted correction.

Keep repeating the process until the major shapes are correct.

Only then add small switches, textures, seams and other details.

You are converting an open-ended task into a sequence of measurable improvements.

Instead of asking Claude to make something that merely looks like a Nova, you are giving it a reference, a specific target and a way to evaluate the result.

For a historically accurate car interior, that is far more useful than generating a complete model from a text prompt.

1. Why Claude can match the diagrams but still get the car wrong

There are five main reasons.

A. Orthographic alignment doesn't guarantee the correct surface

Imagine you have a side-view drawing of the Nova.

Claude builds a body shell and checks it against that drawing. The silhouette lines up perfectly.

Now imagine looking at the car from the front three-quarter angle.

The bonnet might be too wide at the windscreen and too narrow at the nose. The front wings might have the wrong curvature. The shoulders above the wheel arches might be too flat.

None of those errors necessarily shows up in the side-view silhouette.

The same applies to the top view. A body can match the roof outline but have the wrong cross-sectional curvature, window recesses and shoulder lines.

A reference drawing constrains the silhouette, but it doesn't uniquely define the three-dimensional surface between those silhouettes.

This is particularly important for a car body, where relatively subtle changes in curvature can dramatically alter the perceived shape.

B. Claude may be creating the right outline using the wrong geometry

There are several ways to construct a car body in Blender:

Extruding a side profile.

Modelling a collection of individual panels.

Building a surface from cross-sections.

Creating a continuous surface from carefully positioned curves.

Using a subdivision surface workflow.

Constructing surfaces from a network of boundary curves.

These approaches can all produce a model with the correct overall dimensions, but they produce very different surfaces.

For an angular late-1980s hatchback such as the Nova, simply extruding the side profile and smoothing the result is unlikely to produce a convincing body.

The real car has a particular combination of flat-ish panels, subtle curvature, creases, rounded transitions and sharply defined edges.

The modelling method needs to preserve those characteristics.

C. The surface curvature is probably wrong

This is the issue I'd investigate first.

The human eye recognises cars partly through the way light travels across their bodywork.

Consider the Nova's bonnet, front wings, doors and rear quarters. Their identity comes from more than their boundaries. It comes from how their surfaces transition between those boundaries.

If Claude produces a bonnet that is 1,100 mm wide, for example, that doesn't establish whether its centre is flat, crowned or excessively domed.

Likewise, a door can have the correct length and height but still look wrong because its surface curvature and shoulder line are inaccurate.

This explains why a model can pass dimensional checks yet look noticeably unlike the reference.

D. The model may be too smooth or too uniformly rounded

A late-1980s car body isn't a collection of perfectly smooth, inflated surfaces.

It has specific changes in curvature, including:

Relatively flat areas of bonnet and door skin.

Defined transitions around the wheel arches.

Subtle crowns in broad panels.

Changes in surface direction around the wings.

Distinct window frames and recesses.

Sharp or semi-sharp transitions between panels.

If Claude uses subdivision surfaces indiscriminately, it can soften the exact features that make the Nova recognisable.

Conversely, using entirely flat surfaces can make the body look like a low-poly approximation.

The objective is not simply to make the model smooth. It's to make the right parts smooth, the right parts flat, and the right edges sharp.

E. Claude may be judging its own work using the wrong criteria

If Claude reports that the dimensions and reference alignment are correct, it may be evaluating what is easiest to measure rather than what matters most visually.

A useful validation system needs to check more than bounding boxes and silhouette overlays.

It must also examine cross-sections, surface normals, curvature transitions, highlights and three-quarter views.

That's where I'd make the biggest change to your workflow.

2. Change how Claude constructs the body shell

For your Nova, I would move away from a workflow where Claude builds the body, overlays the reference images and declares it correct.

Instead, have it build a surface-driven body model using controlled cross-sections and explicit feature lines.

This is a more technical approach, but it gives Claude much better control over the shapes that determine whether the car looks right.

Method A: Build the body from cross-sections
15

Think of the Nova body as a series of carefully shaped cross-sections running along its length.

At each longitudinal position, you define the body profile from the centreline outwards, describing the height and width of the body at different vertical levels.

For example, you might establish sections at:

Front bumper.

Front of bonnet.

Front axle.

Rear of bonnet.

Windscreen base.

Dashboard and bulkhead region.

B-pillar.

Rear axle.

Rear hatch.

Rear bumper.

These are starting locations, not a claim that the original body has a structural seam at every position.

Each section defines the body shape at that location. A controlled surface is then generated between neighbouring sections.

Why this works better

Suppose the front wing looks wrong.

With a conventional mesh built through extrusion and repeated smoothing, changing the wing's curvature may affect the bonnet, wheel arch and front bumper.

With a cross-section-based approach, you can adjust the relevant sections and the surface between them.

You can also specify additional sections where the geometry changes quickly, such as around the front wheel arch or the transition into the bumper.

The key is that the cross-sections must be derived from the actual car, not generated as evenly spaced generic profiles.

A Nova-specific body needs Nova-specific sections.

Method B: Define the important feature lines first
8

Before generating the complete body surface, have Claude identify the major feature lines of the original car.

These include:

Bonnet perimeter.

Front wing and bonnet boundaries.

Headlamp and grille boundaries.

Front bumper profile.

Wheel-arch lips.

Door shut lines.

Window apertures.

Shoulder and waist lines.

Rear-quarter transitions.

Hatch perimeter.

Rear bumper profile.

These lines establish the character of the car.

They should be modelled as explicit curves or edges rather than left to emerge accidentally from a smoothed mesh.

For example, if the Nova's front wing has a defined transition around the wheel arch, that transition should be represented deliberately. Don't expect a subdivision modifier to discover it.

A useful distinction

There are two separate modelling problems:

Primary form: the bonnet, roof, doors, wings and rear quarters that establish the overall shape.

Secondary form: wheel-arch lips, panel shut lines, bumper seams, trim and other smaller details.

Get the primary form correct first. If the primary surfaces are wrong, adding more panel lines will only make the incorrect model more detailed.

3. Use a hybrid surface workflow, not subdivision everywhere

For your project, I would use different modelling methods for different parts of the body.

Component	Preferred approach	Main reason
Roof	Controlled surface with transverse sections	Maintains the correct crown and roof profile
Bonnet	Surface guided by perimeter curves and cross-sections	Controls the subtle curvature of the panel
Front wings	Cross-sections combined with wheel-arch geometry	Preserves the relationship between the wing and arch
Doors	Defined perimeter and surface profiles	Prevents the panels becoming excessively rounded
Rear quarters	Controlled surfaces between wheel arch, roof and hatch	Maintains the distinctive rear body shape
Bumpers	Separate surfaces or meshes	Makes their shape and attachment easier to control
Wheel arches	Explicit arch curves and surrounding surfaces	Prevents generic, overly circular arches
Window apertures	Defined curves and carefully controlled surrounding geometry	Keeps glass openings and pillars accurate
Door shut lines	Separate curves, grooves or carefully defined edges	Allows adjustment without rebuilding the whole body

This doesn't mean every panel must be a separate, disconnected mesh.

A production-quality body can use multiple connected surfaces, with continuity carefully controlled at the boundaries.

The important thing is to avoid letting a single generic smoothing operation dictate the shape of the whole car.

What about NURBS?

NURBS surfaces and curves are useful because they allow precise control over smooth surface shape using relatively few control points.

However, Blender's NURBS support is not a complete automotive surfacing CAD system. It doesn't automatically solve the problems of maintaining surface continuity, joining panels or producing a clean game-ready mesh.

For your project, a sensible workflow is:

Use curves and cross-sections to establish the intended shape.

Generate or construct controlled surfaces.

Refine the surfaces where necessary.

Convert the result into an appropriate mesh.

Inspect the mesh, normals, topology and shading.

Export and test it in Assetto Corsa.

Use NURBS where they help, not simply because they sound more advanced.
